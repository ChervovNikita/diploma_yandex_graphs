62: Abstract
63: In resource-constrained and low-latency settings, uncertainty estimates must be efficiently obtained.
64: Deep Ensembles provide robust epistemic uncertainty (EU) but require training multiple full-size models.
65: BatchEnsemble aims to deliver ensemble-like EU at far lower parameter and memory cost by applying learned rank-1 perturbations to a shared base network.
66: We show that BatchEnsemble not only underperforms Deep Ensembles but closely tracks a single model baseline in terms of accuracy, calibration and out-of-distribution (OOD) detection on CIFAR-10/10C/SVHN.
67: A controlled study on MNIST finds members are near-identical in function and parameter space, indicating limited capacity to realize distinct predictive modes.
68: Thus, BatchEnsemble behaves more like a single model than a true ensemble.

81: 2 Background and Related Work
82: Deep Ensemble.
83: Deep Ensembles [13] approximate the posterior distribution p⁡(y∣x,D)p(y\mid x,D) of observing label yy for an unseen input xx given the training data DD by uniformly averaging the outputs of kk independently trained models:
84: p^​(y∣x,D)=1k​∑i=1kp⁡(y∣x,θi),\hat{p}(y\mid x,D)=\frac{1}{k}\sum^{k}_{i=1}p(y\mid x,\theta_{i}),
85: (1)
86: where θi\theta_{i} are parameters obtained from different random initialization and training trajectories.
87: Deep Ensembles provide robust epistemic uncertainty (EU) under distribution shift and outperform scalable Bayesian approximations such as stochastic variational inference (SVI) [18].
88: However, the computational and memory cost of Deep Ensembles scales linearly with kk, which makes them impractical for low-latency or resource constrained settings such as high-throughput screening, real-time systems, or large-scale models.
89: BatchEnsemble.
90: To mitigate the linear scaling in computational/memory cost of Deep Ensembles,
91: Wen et al. [19] introduced BatchEnsemble.
92: BatchEnsemble promises to deliver ensemble-level EU at (nearly) single model cost.
93: To this end, BatchEnsemble embeds an ensemble within one network by applying learned low-rank, per-member perturbations to a shared weight matrix.
94: Let W∈ℝm×nW\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} denote a layer’s shared weight and ri∈ℝmr_{i}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{m}, si∈ℝns_{i}\!\in\!\mathbb{R}^{n} be member-specific learned vectors.
95: Then, BatchEnsemble parametrizes the weight matrix WiW_{i} of each member i=1,⋯,ki=1,\cdots,k as
96: Wi=W∘ri​si⊤,W_{i}=W\circ r_{i}s_{i}^{\top},
97: (2)
98: where ∘\circ denotes the Hadamard product and each ri​si⊤r_{i}s_{i}^{\top} is a rank-1 matrix.
99: BatchEnsemble thus reduces the per-layer parameters from O⁡(k​m​n)O(kmn) for independent members obtained using Deep Ensembles, to O⁡(m​n+k⁡(m+n))O(mn+k(m+n)).
100: This leads to a substantial parameter reduction, especially for large matrices where k≪min⁡(m,n)k\!\ll\!\min(m,n).
101: The forward pass for a single input x∈ℝnx\in\mathbb{R}^{n} can be written as
102: Wi​x=(W∘ri​si⊤)=W⁡(si∘x)∘ri,W_{i}x=(W\circ r_{i}s_{i}^{\top})=W(s_{i}\circ x)\circ r_{i},
103: (3)
104: with member-specific biases omitted for brevity.
105: This formulation admits a vectorized implementation (Appendix A) that computes the prediction of all kk members in a single batched forward pass—hence “BatchEnsemble”—enabling within-device parallelism and high-throughput inference.
106: By contrast the popular ensemble alternative MC Dropout [6] requires multiple stochastic forward passes to obtain an ensemble of predictions.
107: Ensemble Diversity and EU.
108: A leading explanation for the success of Deep Ensembles is that independently trained members explore distinct solutions not only in parameter space but also in function space [5].
109: In contrast, scalable Bayesian methods such as mean-field VI [7], Bayes by Backprop [2], or MC Dropout [6] approximate uncertainty by placing a simple distribution (e.g., diagonal Gaussian or dropout mask) centered around a single optimum.
110: Consequently, efficient ensembling methods should be designed such that they are able to replicate this effect by capturing distinct predictive modes, not just local perturbations around a single optimum.
111: This work.
112: We compare BatchEnsemble to Deep Ensembles and MC Dropout on CIFAR-10 across in-distribution, distribution shift, and out-of-distribution (OOD) settings (Section 4; protocols and metrics in Appendix D.1 and C).
113: BatchEnsemble does not deliver ensemble-level gains, yielding low epistemic uncertainty and closely matching the single-model baseline in terms of predictive performance, calibration, and OOD detection.
114: A diagnostic MNIST study further reveals BatchEnsemble members are nearly identical in parameter space and function space, implying that limited capacity to realize multiple predictive modes may underlie BatchEnsemble’s limited EU.
115: 3 Multiplicative Rank-1 Perturbations Have Measure Zero
116: We begin by making some simple but pertinent theoretical observations that show that the parameter space expressible by BatchEnsembles is vanishingly small compared to Deep Ensembles.
117: Observation 1.
118: Consider ensembles with kk members.
119: For BatchEnsemble, consider the members as parameterized by ri∈ℝmr_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m}, si∈ℝns_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n}, and the shared weight matrix WW.
120: Let 𝒲BE={(W∘r1s1⊤,…,W∘rksk⊤)∣ri∈ℝm,si∈ℝn,i∈[k],W∈ℝm×n}\mathcal{W}_{\text{BE}}=\{\left(W\circ r_{1}s_{1}^{\top},\dots,W\circ r_{k}s_{k}^{\top}\right)\mid r_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m},s_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n},i\in[k],W\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}\} be the set of weights achievable with BatchEnsemble.
121: Let 𝒲DE={(W1,…,Wk)∣Wi∈ℝm×n,i∈[k]}\mathcal{W}_{\text{DE}}=\{\left(W_{1},\dots,W_{k}\right)\mid W_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n},i\in[k]\} be the set of weights achievable with Deep Ensembles, where each ensemble member has an independent weight matrix layer WiW_{i} of size ℝm×n\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}.
122: Then, 𝒲BE⊊𝒲DE\mathcal{W}_{\text{BE}}\subsetneq\mathcal{W}_{\text{DE}}. Moreover, 𝒲BE\mathcal{W}_{\text{BE}} has measure zero in 𝒲DE\mathcal{W}_{\text{DE}}.
123: A proof of Observation 1 and examples of weight matrices unrepresented weight matrices are provided in Appendix B.
124: This result shows that the weights reachable by BatchEnsemble’s rank-1 multiplicative perturbations constitute a strict, measure-zero subset of those reachable by independently trained members, i.e., Deep Ensembles.
125: This limited expressivity suggests that BatchEnsemble cannot capture the same degree of diversity as a Deep Ensemble.
126: In the next sections, we test this hypothesis empirically on real data.

243: 5 BatchEnsemble Is Not Diverse
244: The results provided in the previous section suggest that BatchEnsemble behaves more like a single model than an ensemble.
245: If this stems from insufficient member diversity, we should observe it in a controlled setting with minimal confounds.
246: We therefore analyze diversity in a simple 3-layer perceptron on MNIST with k=4k{=}4 members (Appendix D.2), where perturbations apply to linear layers only.
247: This choice removes channel–rescaling effects in CNNs (Appendix F).
248: The architecture can therefore neither mask diversity in BatchEnsemble, nor induce it in Deep Ensembles, allowing for a fair comparison of ensemble diversity.
249: We assess diversity in both function and parameter space.
250: Let fθ:𝒳→𝒴f_{\theta}:\mathcal{X}\!\to\!\mathcal{Y} denote a neural network predictor with weights θ\theta.
251: In function space, we use pairwise prediction disagreement on {xi}i=1N\{x_{i}\}_{i=1}^{N}:
252: Disagreement(fθ1,fθ2)=1N∑i=1N[fθ1(xi)≠fθ2(xi)],\mathrm{Disagreement}(f_{\theta_{1}},f_{\theta_{2}})=\tfrac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\!\left[f_{\theta_{1}}(x_{i})\neq f_{\theta_{2}}(x_{i})\right],
253: In parameter space, we compare the (vectorized) weights via cosine similarity:
254: cos⁡(θ1,θ2)=⟨θ1,θ2⟩‖θ1‖​‖θ2‖.\cos(\theta_{1},\theta_{2})=\frac{\langle\theta_{1},\theta_{2}\rangle}{\|\theta_{1}\|\,\|\theta_{2}\|}.
255: Since BatchEnsemble defines members implicitly through input/output scalings per layer (Eq. 3), we form explicit weight tensors using Eq. 2 before computing cosine similarity.

268: Together, these results indicate limited capacity of BatchEnsemble to realize multiple predictive modes, consistent with its near-zero epistemic uncertainty in Section 4.
269: 6 Conclusion
270: BatchEnsemble is designed to provide ensemble-like uncertainty at near single-model cost, yet in our setting it failed to reproduce the behaviour of Deep Ensembles.
271: Across CIFAR-10/10C/SVHN, its predictive performance and calibration closely tracked a single model, and its epistemic uncertainty remained low.
272: A controlled MNIST study further revealed near-zero functional disagreement and near-unity weight-space similarity, indicating limited ability to realize distinct predictive modes.
273: To support a fuller assessment of efficient ensemble-like UQ methods, we recommend reporting uncertainty measures such as predictive entropy (H) and Jensen–Shannon divergence (JSD) alongside standard accuracy, calibration, and OOD metrics.
274: Simple diagnostics of functional and weight-space diversity, as used here, can additionally help determine whether a method expresses genuinely distinct predictive modes or merely produces correlated variants of a single model.
275: 7 Limitations & Future Work
276: Our findings differ from the stronger BatchEnsemble results reported by Wen et al. [19].
277: This discrepancy is largely attributable to scale 11
278: 1
279: Wen et al. [19] used wider and deeper networks trained for substantially longer (e.g., ResNet-32×4, 250–375 epochs), whereas our evaluation followed the lighter protocol of Ovadia et al. [18] with ResNet-18 trained for 75 epochs.
280: The improved single-model performance reported in Wen et al. [19] relative to the Ovadia benchmark suggests that much of the reported gain is driven by increased capacity rather than ensembling-specific effects.
281: Unfortunately, their benchmark does not report uncertainty measures such as H or JSD and does not analyse functional or weight-space diversity.
282: .
283: Although larger models can narrow the performance gap, scaling and ensembling offer fundamentally different advantages under equal parameter budgets [15].
284: Thus, a natural next step is to measure BatchEnsemble’s diversity and epistemic uncertainty under increased width, depth, and training time, extending this analysis to the exact settings used by Wen et al. [19].

419: Appendix A BatchEnsemble Vectorization
420: To enable a vectorized forward pass across all kk ensemble members, Wen et al. [19] constrain the mini-batch size bb to be divisible by kk such that each ensemble member processes a disjoint subset of b/kb/k samples.
421: The input matrix X∈ℝb×nX\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times n} is split accordingly, and the corresponding kk rank-1 scaling vectors ri∈ℝmr_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{m} and si∈ℝns_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{n} are tiled b/kb/k times to form the matrices R∈ℝb×mR\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times m} and S∈ℝb×nS\in\mathbb{R}^{b\times n}, which contain the row and column scaling factors, respectively.
422: This allows the forward pass for the full mini-batch to be written as:
423: ((S∘X)​W⊤)∘R,\bigl((S\circ X)W^{\top}\bigr)\circ R,
424: (4)
425: where W∈ℝm×nW\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n} is the shared weight matrix, and Hadamard products are applied row-wise.
426: While this formulation is computationally efficient, each ensemble member only sees a fraction 1/k1/k of the data in a given iteration.
427: Alternatively, the mini-batch can be expanded such that each input sample is repeated kk times, once for each ensemble member.
428: This results in an input matrix X∈ℝb​k×nX\in\mathbb{R}^{bk\times n}, where each original sample appears kk times.
429: The scaling matrices R∈ℝb​k×mR\in\mathbb{R}^{bk\times m} and S∈ℝb​k×nS\in\mathbb{R}^{bk\times n} are constructed by repeating each ensemble member’s vectors rir_{i} and sis_{i} over the entire batch.
430: This ensures that each ensemble member processes all data in every training iteration, at the cost of increased training time due to input duplication (see Appendix E).
