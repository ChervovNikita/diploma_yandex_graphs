76: Abstract
77: While parameter-efficient fine-tuning methods like low-rank adaptation (LoRA) are standard for large language models, principled estimation of epistemic uncertainty remains challenging. Recent results in the LoRA regime suggest that discrete multi-mode approaches such as deep ensembles offer little benefit over single-mode methods. This contradicts broader observations in deep learning, where ensembling independent optima typically improves generalization, and linking these modes through continuous low-loss valleys further enhances Bayesian model averaging (BMA). Whether such structure exists in the LoRA space and whether it yields functional diversity missed by local or discrete methods has not been studied. We introduce LoRA-Curve, a segmented Bézier curve parameterization in the LoRA space, with two variants: a free configuration that jointly optimizes all control points, and an anchored configuration that connects independently fine-tuned LoRA optima.
78: We prove pathwise continuity and Lipschitz regularity of the loss along the curve and empirically show, across reasoning and classification benchmarks with Qwen2.5 7B, that linear interpolation encounters loss barriers, while our anchored multi-segment curves connect independent optima through continuous low-loss valleys. Combined with flat-minima perturbations and a Jensen–Shannon divergence regularizer, LoRA-Curve yields measurably higher mutual information of the predictive distribution without sacrificing performance, and links continuous parameter-space traversal to functional diversity.

107: Bayesian parameter-efficient fine-tuning
108: Classic approaches for capturing predictive uncertainty in deep learning rely on variational inference (VI) [2, 16, see, e.g.,], the Laplace approximation [5, e.g.,], and deep ensembles (DEs) [12].
109: To scale these techniques to modern LLMs, recent work has integrated uncertainty estimation in the LoRA parameterization. Prominent variational examples include Bayesian Low-Rank Adaptation by Backpropagation (BLoB) [19], which learns a variational distribution over the AA matrix via backpropagation, and Scalable Bayesian Low-Rank Adaptation (ScalaBL) [18], which utilizes further weight factorization to drastically reduce the stochastic parameter count for improved scalability. Other approaches include discrete LoRA ensembles to approximate the posterior [1, 20].
110: Loss landscapes and mode connectivity
111: While the previously mentioned approaches successfully capture epistemic uncertainty at a fraction of the full-parameter cost, their geometric assumptions are in stark contrast (single- vs. multi-mode) and conflict with each other regarding the geometry of the posterior or loss landscape. In general neural network applications, it has been shown that independent neural network optima are rarely isolated, but rather often form connected and continuous paths of low loss [7, 8]. The analysis of low-loss valleys could therefore provide insights into the geometrical structure of LoRA posterior spaces, but so far, subspace methods have not been (successfully) applied to LoRA.
112: 3 Constructing a curve-subspace for LoRA-based inference
113: We demonstrate how to efficiently construct continuous, low-loss valleys within the LoRA weight space. To this end, we introduce LoRA-Curve, a segmented Bézier parameterization for the LoRA space. Depending on the initialization and optimization of the anchor control points of the curve, we will distinguish between two configurations: Free LoRA-Curve, where all control points are jointly optimized, and Anchored LoRA-Curve, where segment boundaries (anchor points) are frozen to independently pretrained adapters (see Figure 1).
114: 3.1 Free LoRA-Curve
115: Building on the flexibility of different parameterizations discussed in Garipov et al. [8], we construct the LoRA-Curve using a Bézier curve with m+2m+2 control points. Specifically for the LoRA fine-tuning setting, we parameterize a continuous path t↦ζϑ​(t)∈Θt\mapsto\zeta_{\vartheta}(t)\in\Theta for t∈[0,1]t\in[0,1] using

132: 3.2 Segmented curves
133: To navigate highly non-convex loss landscapes, a single polynomial segment is often insufficiently flexible (cf. Figure 5(a)). We therefore extend the LoRA-Curve framework to a composite Bézier curve comprising multiple individual segments.
134: To construct this composite path, we define two distinct types of control points: anchors, which act as segment boundaries and lie directly on the curve, and handles, which are intermediate points that shape the curve but generally do not lie on it (cf. Figure 1).
135: Let NN denote the number of anchors and mm the number of handles per segment.
136: For a curve with NN anchor points connected by Nseg=N−1N_{\text{seg}}=N-1 segments, each with mm handles, the global parameter is t∈[0,Nseg]t\in[0,N_{\text{seg}}] and the total number of control points is Ncp=Nseg​(m+1)+1N_{\text{cp}}=N_{\text{seg}}(m+1)+1. The active segment index is determined via k=min⁡(⌊t⌋,N−2)∈{0,…,N−2}k=\min(\lfloor t\rfloor,N-2)\in\{0,\dots,N-2\} with local coordinate τ=t−k∈[0,1]\tau=t-k\in[0,1]. We then evaluate the curve locally using this subset of control points
137: ζϑ​(τ)=∑i=0m+1bi,m+1​(τ)​θk⁡(m+1)+i.\zeta_{\vartheta}(\tau)=\sum_{i=0}^{m+1}b_{i,m+1}(\tau)\,\theta_{k(m+1)+i}.
138: This piecewise construction ensures that adjacent segments share exactly one boundary control point (an anchor point). This construction guarantees C0C^{0} continuity while permitting sharp corners, allowing the path to flexibly bend around steep loss barriers (cf. the bend at θ3\theta_{3} in Figure 1). By contrast, a single Bézier curve of degree (Ncp−1)(N_{\text{cp}}-1) is globally coupled: every control point influences the entire curve via its Bernstein basis function, making localized bends difficult and the path overly rigid when navigating highly non-convex valleys.
139: 3.3 Anchored LoRA-Curve
140: While FLC optimizes all control points (the NN anchor points and all Nseg​mN_{\text{seg}}m handles) from scratch, we can explicitly guide the curve to connect known high-performing optima (e.g., those obtained from a Deep Ensemble) by freezing the anchors to the weights of independently fine-tuned LoRA models.
141: In this scenario, the remaining handles (initialized exactly as in FLC to explicitly increase diversity) are updated via gradient descent according to Equation 2.
142: We call this approach Anchored LoRA-curve (ALC) and its configuration as ALC​(N,m)\text{ALC}(N,m), where NN is the number of frozen anchors and mm the number of handles per segment. Analogously, FLC​(N,m)\text{FLC}(N,m) denotes the free variant where the anchors and handles are jointly optimized.
143: This unified notation mathematically subsumes several standard methods as special cases: MAP corresponds to FLC​(1,0)\text{FLC}(1,0) (a single point estimate with no curve); DE​(N)\text{DE}(N) corresponds to NN independent anchors with m=0m=0; and ALC​(N,0)\text{ALC}(N,0) reduces to standard piecewise linear mode connectivity between NN optima, which we denote as Lin​(N)\text{Lin}(N).
144: 3.4 Diversity regularization
145: To encourage functional diversity along the path and prevent the curve from collapsing into a single point-estimate, we employ a margin-based Jensen-Shannon Divergence (JSD) regularizer. Let f​(x,Wϑ​(t))f(x;W_{\vartheta}(t)) denote the model logits for input xx at curve parameter t∈[0,Nseg]t\in[0,N_{\text{seg}}], and let pt:=softmax⁡(f⁡(x,Wϑ​(t)))p_{t}:=\operatorname{softmax}(f(x;W_{\vartheta}(t))) denote the resulting predictive distribution.

206: 5.2 Implications of low-loss valleys design on the performance
207: Figure 4 presents a direct performance comparison between our curve approaches and discrete DEs. Because ALC uses the exact same anchors as DE, it provides a controlled
208: framework that isolates both the effect of continuous versus discrete averaging (comparing DE​(N)\text{DE}(N) to Lin​(N)\text{Lin}(N)) and the necessity of non-linear routing (comparing ALC​(N,m)\text{ALC}(N,m) to Lin​(N)\text{Lin}(N)).
209: The linear formulation (ALC with m=0m=0) proves too restrictive, failing to capture the curvature of the underlying low-loss manifold. By contrast, the higher-order ALC​(N,2)\text{ALC}(N,2) successfully matches or outperforms DE across datasets.
210: FLC, however, exhibits less consistent gains, struggling particularly on the winogrande_m dataset. This performance gap is intriguing because FLC and ALC possess identical representational capacity; in principle, FLC can represent any curve that ALC can. The discrepancy, therefore, stems strictly from the optimization strategy. While jointly optimizing all control points makes FLC significantly faster to train overall (cf. Table 4), it induces fundamentally different optimization dynamics compared to the sequential ALC process.
211: Furthermore, this exposes a prominent generalization gap. Although Figure 2 demonstrates that FLC discovers smooth, low-loss valleys in the training data, this does not consistently translate into superior test performance, indicating a misalignment between the training and test landscapes (cf. Figure 8).
212: Despite these optimization challenges, increasing the number of anchors NN generally improves performance for all methods (DE, ALC, and FLC). This suggests that multiple modes, as captured by longer paths, result in better performance regardless of the specific training regimen.

222: 6 Conclusion
223: In this work, we introduced LoRA-Curve, a segmented Bézier parameterization designed to investigate the continuous posterior geometry of LLMs fine-tuned via low-rank adaptation. Contrary to recent suggestions that the LoRA landscape lacks beneficial multi-mode structure, our findings demonstrate that the space between independent optima is a rich, navigable region of low loss.
224: Our free (FLC) and anchored (ALC) configurations successfully construct meaningful, continuous low-loss valleys that bypass steep loss barriers.
225: Furthermore, these two variants offer valuable practical trade-offs: FLC provides an efficient optimization approach by dynamically discovering valleys from scratch, whereas ALC enables the explicit, targeted connection of independently fine-tuned optima.
226: Navigating these non-convex valleys captures unique functional diversity. Unlike local variational methods that explore only a single basin, or discrete DEs that aggregate isolated point estimates, LoRA-Curve guarantees smooth predictive transitions and explicitly links continuous parameter-space traversal to measurably higher mutual information without sacrificing predictive performance.
227: For probabilistic LoRA applications, our results establish that the continuous space between modes serves as a viable manifold for BMA, with functional diversity scaling naturally alongside the number of curve segments.
228: Combining this parameterization with flat-minima perturbations broadens the loss valley occupied by the curve, while JSD regularization further increases functional diversity along it.
229: Limitations
230: While the LoRA-Curve framework provides a principled approach to continuous mode connectivity, it presents certain structural and practical limitations. First, we restrict our analysis to one-dimensional, globally coupled trajectories. Extending this parameterization to higher-dimensional surfaces or assigning independent curves to individual LoRA adapters would severely degrade interpretability; our preliminary experiments also confirmed that the latter approach yields no empirical performance gains. Furthermore, while traditional BMA over continuous manifolds faces a significant computational bottleneck, we established that computing posterior weights on the training dataset in our setting leads to detrimental double-conditioning.
231: Consequently, our reliance on uniform weighting (T→∞T\to\infty) is a practical choice that is empirically validated and motivated by a double-counting argument (see Appendix B), conveniently bypassing this bottleneck in our one-dimensional case.
232: However, in higher-dimensional parameterizations, uniform weighting would likely prove insufficient, thereby reintroducing the prohibitive cost of evaluating the full posterior. Finally, while the Free configuration (FLC) accelerates optimization, it currently exhibits a prominent generalization gap.

961: E.1 Repulsive regularization
962: Early in the development of the ALC framework, we hypothesized that enforcing geometric diversity directly in the parameter space would prevent mode collapse. To this end, we introduced a repulsive regularizer that penalized the pairwise cosine similarity between the curve’s control points, ϑi\vartheta_{i}. For NN control points, this regularizer was defined as the sum of squared cosine similarities over all distinct pairs:
963: ℛrepulsive=∑1≤i<j≤Ncp(ϑi⋅ϑj‖ϑi‖​‖ϑj‖)2.\mathcal{R}_{\text{repulsive}}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq N_{\mathrm{cp}}}\left(\frac{\vartheta_{i}\cdot\vartheta_{j}}{\|\vartheta_{i}\|\|\vartheta_{j}\|}\right)^{2}.
964: While this explicitly forced the control points to disperse across the LoRA space, it yielded inconsistent downstream performance. Crucially, geometric distance in weight space did not strongly correlate with functional diversity in the model’s predictive distributions. Instead, strong repulsive forces often pushed the control points into higher-loss regions, causing the trajectory to deviate from the optimal low-loss valley.
965: Consequently, this weight-space regularization was discarded in favor of output-space regularization (such as the JSD regularizer described in Section 3.4), which directly encourages diversity in the predictive distributions without unnecessarily distorting the underlying geometric path.
