3 Methodology

In this section, we present the Counterfactual Routing (CoR) framework. We first formalize the standard Sparse Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) architecture. We then detail our two-phase approach: an offline causal analysis to identify critical components (Figure 1), and an online inference strategy based on compute-preserving expert redistribution (Figure 2).

3.1 Preliminaries

We consider a Transformer-based MoE model with LL layers. Let 𝐡l−1(t)∈ℝd\mathbf{h}_{l-1}^{(t)}\in\mathbb{R}^{d} denote the hidden state of the tt-th token at layer l−1l-1. The ll-th MoE layer consists of NN expert networks {El,i}i=1N\{E_{l,i}\}_{i=1}^{N}, where each expert is typically a Feed-Forward Network (FFN).

Gating Mechanism. A router (or gating network) GlG_{l} determines the contribution of each expert. It typically applies a linear projection followed by a Softmax function:

Gl​(𝐡l−1(t))=Softmax​(𝐡l−1(t)​𝐖g)G_{l}(\mathbf{h}_{l-1}^{(t)})=\text{Softmax}(\mathbf{h}_{l-1}^{(t)}\mathbf{W}_{g}) (1)

where 𝐖g∈ℝd×N\mathbf{W}_{g}\in\mathbb{R}^{d\times N} is the learnable routing weight matrix. Let gl,i(t)g_{l,i}^{(t)} denote the gating probability for the ii-th expert. To ensure sparsity and computational efficiency, standard MoE employs a Top-kk selection strategy. The set of active indices for token tt is defined as 𝒜l(t)=Top-​k​({gl,i(t)}i=1N)\mathcal{A}_{l}^{(t)}=\text{Top-}k(\{g_{l,i}^{(t)}\}_{i=1}^{N}), where k≪Nk\ll N.

Sparse Output. The output of the MoE layer, 𝐡l(t)\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(t)}, is the weighted sum of the activated experts’ outputs plus the residual connection:

𝐡l(t)=𝐡l−1(t)+∑i∈𝒜l(t)gl,i(t)⋅El,i​(𝐡l−1(t))\mathbf{h}_{l}^{(t)}=\mathbf{h}_{l-1}^{(t)}+\sum_{i\in\mathcal{A}_{l}^{(t)}}g_{l,i}^{(t)}\cdot E_{l,i}(\mathbf{h}_{l-1}^{(t)}) (2)

3.2 Offline Causal Analysis

Standard routing mechanisms often fail to capture long-tail knowledge due to spurious correlations learned during training. To rectify this, we perform a comprehensive offline analysis on a calibration dataset to pinpoint where factual knowledge resides (Layer-wise analysis) and which experts are causally necessary (Expert-wise analysis).

3.2.1 Token Stratification

To disentangle knowledge-intensive reasoning from generic syntactic processing, we stratify tokens based on model uncertainty. By performing a forward pass on a calibration dataset (e.g., C4 (Dodge et al., 2021)) using the Original model ℳ\mathcal{M}, we compute the token-level negative log-likelihood (NLL) loss ℓ(t)\ell^{(t)}. We define two contrastive subsets based on percentile thresholds: the hard sample set 𝒟hard={t∣ℓ(t)>τhigh}\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}=\{t\mid\ell^{(t)}>\tau_{\text{high}}\} representing long-tail knowledge, and the easy sample set 𝒟easy={t∣ℓ(t)<τlow}\mathcal{D}_{\text{easy}}=\{t\mid\ell^{(t)}<\tau_{\text{low}}\} representing common syntax.

3.2.2 Layer-wise analysis

Standard MoEs allocate a uniform computational budget across all layers. However, we hypothesize that factual knowledge is concentrated in specific deep layers. To locate these layers, we employ Layer-wise Perturbation Analysis.

Crucially, raw perturbation scores are often biased by the “Error Cascading” effect: a small perturbation in shallow layers undergoes successive amplifications through the network’s depth (via matrix multiplications and non-linearities), resulting in a spuriously high loss deviation even for semantically insensitive layers. To eliminate this structural bias and isolate true knowledge dependency, we propose Contrastive Sensitivity Normalization.

Raw Sensitivity Calculation. For a specific layer ll, we apply a uniform multiplicative perturbation δ\delta to its output 𝐎l(t)\mathbf{O}_{l}^{(t)} while keeping other layers frozen. We calculate the expected loss degradation for both hard and easy subsets separately:

Sl​(𝒟)=1|𝒟|​∑t∈𝒟[ℓ⁡(ℳ⁡(𝐎~l(t)))−ℓ(t)]S_{l}(\mathcal{D})=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{D}|}\sum_{t\in\mathcal{D}}\left[\ell(\mathcal{M}(\tilde{\mathbf{O}}_{l}^{(t)}))-\ell^{(t)}\right] (3)

where 𝐎~l(t)=(1+δ)⊙𝐎l(t)\tilde{\mathbf{O}}_{l}^{(t)}=(1+\delta)\odot\mathbf{O}_{l}^{(t)} and 𝒟∈{𝒟hard,𝒟easy}\mathcal{D}\in\{\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}},\mathcal{D}_{\text{easy}}\}. Intuitively, Sl​(𝒟easy)S_{l}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{easy}}) captures the layer’s inherent structural amplification factor, as easy tokens primarily rely on syntax processing rather than deep knowledge retrieval.

Relative Knowledge Intensity (RKI). To decouple the knowledge component from structural noise, we define the final layer score RlR_{l} as the ratio of hard-to-easy sensitivity:

Rl=Sl​(𝒟hard)Sl​(𝒟easy)+ϵR_{l}=\frac{S_{l}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}})}{S_{l}(\mathcal{D}_{\text{easy}})+\epsilon} (4)

A high RlR_{l} indicates that layer ll is disproportionately critical for hard tokens compared to easy ones, signifying a Knowledge-Intensive Layer. This score will guide our Layer-wise analysis budget reallocation. We provide a rigorous theoretical derivation demonstrating how RlR_{l} cancels out structural depth bias in Appendix A.1.

3.2.3 Expert-wise analysis

Within knowledge-intensive layers, the router may still assign low gating scores to certain experts for specific input tokens, even though these experts prove critical when activated on other hard tokens. We term such contextually suppressed experts as “dormant” and propose Counterfactual Expert Impact (CEI), a causal metric that quantifies an expert’s aggregated causal value across the calibration set via virtual ablation.

Specifically, for a target expert ee in layer ll, we first isolate the subset of hard tokens where this expert was originally activated by the Top-kk mechanism, denoted as 𝒯l,e={t∈𝒟hard∣e∈𝒜l(t)}\mathcal{T}_{l,e}=\{t\in\mathcal{D}_{\text{hard}}\mid e\in\mathcal{A}_{l}^{(t)}\}. To quantify the causal contribution of expert ee, we construct a locally counterfactual scenario by explicitly ablating it from the network. To ensure a valid probability distribution during this intervention, the original gating probability mass of expert ee is redistributed proportionally among the remaining experts to form a surrogate distribution g~\tilde{g}:

g~l,j(t)={0if ​j=egl,j(t)∑m≠egl,m(t)if ​j≠e\tilde{g}_{l,j}^{(t)}=\begin{cases}0&\text{if }j=e\\ \frac{g_{l,j}^{(t)}}{\sum_{m\neq e}g_{l,m}^{(t)}}&\text{if }j\neq e\end{cases} (5)

We then compute the counterfactual loss ℓ~(l,−e)(t)\tilde{\ell}_{(l,-e)}^{(t)} using this ablated routing configuration.

The marginal causal effect of expert ee on token tt is defined as the performance degradation (loss increase) caused by its removal, termed the “Rescue Gain”:

Δ​ℓl,e(t)=ℓ~(l,−e)(t)−ℓ(t)\Delta\ell_{l,e}^{(t)}=\tilde{\ell}_{(l,-e)}^{(t)}-\ell^{(t)} (6)

A positive Δ​ℓl,e(t)\Delta\ell_{l,e}^{(t)} implies that expert ee possesses unique knowledge required for token tt that cannot be compensated by other generalist experts. Finally, the global CEI score for expert ee is derived by aggregating the expected causal effect across all relevant hard tokens:

CEIl,e=𝔼t∈𝒯l,e​[Δ​ℓl,e(t)]\text{CEI}_{l,e}=\mathbb{E}_{t\in\mathcal{T}_{l,e}}\left[\Delta\ell_{l,e}^{(t)}\right] (7)

This metric effectively disentangles an expert’s necessity from its popularity (gating frequency), highlighting experts that are critical for correctness despite low routing confidence. Since CEI aggregates causal impact across calibration tokens, an expert with high CEI may still receive a low router score on a new input due to context shift—precisely the “dormant” scenario where CoR intervenes.

3.3 Compute-Preserving Inference

During the inference phase, we dynamically adjust the routing strategy based on the pre-computed layer sensitivity {Rl}\{R_{l}\} and expert criticality {CEIl,e}\{\text{CEI}_{l,e}\}. Our approach operates on the principle of compute-preserving expert redistribution, ensuring that the total computational cost remains constant while maximizing factual accuracy. The inference process is depicted in Figure 2.

Adaptive Budgeting via Reallocation. Instead of a uniform Top-kk across all layers, we reallocate the total computational budget Ktotal=L×kbaselineK_{\text{total}}=L\times k_{\text{baseline}} across layers proportional to their Relative Knowledge Intensity RlR_{l}. The number of active experts klk_{l} for layer ll is calculated as:

kl=Round​(Ktotal⋅Rl∑j=1LRj)k_{l}=\text{Round}\left(K_{\text{total}}\cdot\frac{R_{l}}{\sum_{j=1}^{L}R_{j}}\right) (8)

This strategy allocates more experts to deep, knowledge-intensive layers to enhance reasoning capabilities, while pruning activations in shallow, syntax-dominant layers.11 1 Here, kk denotes the fixed number of active experts employed in the standard Top-kk routing baseline. Crucially, the constraint ∑kl≈Ktotal\sum k_{l}\approx K_{\text{total}} ensures that the overall FLOPs count remains equivalent to the standard baseline.

Awakening Routing via Context-Prior Fusion. Once the layer-wise budget klk_{l} is determined, we rely on a hybrid mechanism to select specific experts. Relying solely on the static CEI score would ignore the dynamic context of the current input token (e.g., syntax structure), potentially harming general fluency. Conversely, relying solely on the original router Gl​(x)G_{l}(x) leads to the dormant expert problem. Therefore, we select the Top-klk_{l} experts by fusing the dynamic context with the causal prior, as illustrated in Figure 3. The final selection score for expert ii in layer ll is defined as:

Scorel,i​(x)=Gl​(x)i⏟Context+λ⋅CEIl,i⏟Prior\text{Score}_{l,i}(x)=\underbrace{G_{l}(x)_{i}}_{\text{Context}}+\lambda\cdot\underbrace{\text{CEI}_{l,i}}_{\text{Prior}} (9)

where λ\lambda is a hyperparameter controlling the intervention strength. Note that to align the scales, we apply Min-Max Normalization to the raw CEIl\text{CEI}_{l} scores within each layer before fusion. This formulation acts as a causal rectification: for general tokens, the high magnitude of Gl​(x)G_{l}(x) dominates, preserving the model’s linguistic capabilities; for hard knowledge tokens where the router is uncertain, the high CEI acts as a bias term to “awaken” the specialist expert.
