[1] h1  Splitting Steepest Descent for Growing Neural Architectures
[2] p abstract1.1 We develop a progressive training approach for neural networks which adaptively grows the network structure by splitting existing neurons to multiple off-springs. By leveraging a functional steepest descent idea, we derive a simple criterion for deciding the best subset of neurons to split and a splitting gradient for optimally updating the off-springs. Theoretically, our splitting strategy is a second-order functional steepest descent for escaping saddle points in an ∞ \infty -Wasserstein metric space, on which the standard parametric gradient descent is a first-order steepest descent. Our method provides a new practical approach for optimizing neural network structures, especially for learning lightweight neural architectures in resource-constrained settings.
[3] h2  1 Introduction
[4] p S1.p1.1 Deep neural networks (DNNs) have achieved remarkable empirical successes recently. However, efficient and automatic optimization of model architectures remains to be a key challenge. Compared with parameter optimization which has been well addressed by gradient-based methods (a.k.a. back-propagation), optimizing model structures involves significantly more challenging discrete optimization with large search spaces and high evaluation cost. Although there have been rapid progresses recently, designing the best architectures still requires a lot of expert knowledge and trial-and-errors for most practical tasks.
[5] p S1.p2.1 This work targets extending the power of gradient descent to the domain of model structure optimization of neural networks. In particular, we consider the problem of progressively growing a neural network by “splitting” existing neurons into several “off-springs”, and develop a simple and practical approach for deciding the best subset of neurons to split and how to split them, adaptively based on the existing model structure. We derive the optimal splitting strategies by considering the steepest descent of the loss when the off-springs are infinitesimally close to the original neurons, yielding a splitting steepest descent that monotonically decrease the loss in the space of model structures.
[6] p S1.p3.1 Our main method, shown in Algorithm 1 , alternates between a standard parametric descent phase which updates the parameters to minimize the loss with a fixed model structure, and a splitting phase which updates the model structures by splitting neurons. The splitting phase is triggered when no further improvement can be made by only updating parameters, and allow us to escape the parametric local optima by augmenting the neural network in a locally optimal fashion. Theoretically, these two phases can be viewed as performing functional steepest descent on an ∞ \infty -Wasserstein metric space, in which the splitting phase is a second-order descent for escaping saddle points in the functional space, while the parametric gradient descent corresponds to a first-order descent . Empirically, our algorithm is simple and practical, and provides a promising tool for many challenging problems, including progressive training of interpretable neural networks, learning lightweight and energy-efficient neural architectures for resource-constrained settings, and transfer learning, etc.
[7] h4  Related Works
[8] p S1.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p1.1 The idea of progressively growing neural networks by node splitting is not new, but previous works are mostly based on heuristic or purely random splitting strategies ( Wynne-Jones, 1992 ; Chen et al., 2016 , e.g.,) . A different approach for progressive training is the Frank-Wolfe or gradient boosting based strategies ( Schwenk & Bengio, 2000 ; Bengio et al., 2006 ; Bach, 2017 , e.g.,) , which iteratively add new neurons derived from functional conditional gradient, while keeping the previous neurons fixed. However, these methods are not suitable for large scale settings, because adding each neuron requires to solve a difficult non-convex optimization problem, and keeping the previous neurons fixed prevents us from correcting the mistakes made in earlier iterations. A practical alternative of Frank-Wolfe is to simply add new randomly initialized neurons and co-optimize the new and old neurons together. However, random initialization does not allow us to leverage the information of the existing model and takes more time to converge. In contrast, splitting neurons from the existing network allows us to inherent the knowledge from the existing model (see Chen et al. (2016) ), and is faster to converge in settings like continual learning, when the previous model is not far away from the optimal solution.
[9] p S1.SS0.SSS0.Px1.p2.1 An opposite direction of progressive training is to prune large pre-trained networks ( Han et al., 2016 ; Li et al., 2017 ; Liu et al., 2017 , e.g.,) . In comparison, our splitting method requires no large pre-trained models and can outperform existing pruning methods in terms of learning ultra-small neural architectures, which is of critical importance for resource-constrained settings like mobile devices and Internet of things. More broadly, there has been a series of recent works on neural architecture search, based on various strategies from combinatorial optimization, including reinforcement learning (RL) ( Pham et al., 2018 ; Cai et al., 2018 ; Zoph & Le, 2017 , e.g.,) , evolutionary algorithms (EA) ( Stanley & Miikkulainen, 2002 ; Real et al., 2018 , e.g.,) , and continuous relaxation ( Liu et al., 2019a ; Xie et al., 2018 , e.g.,) . However, these general-purpose black-box optimization methods do not leverage the inherent geometric structure of the loss landscape, and are highly computationally expensive due to the need of evaluating the candidate architectures based on inner training loops.
[10] h4  Background: Steepest Descent and Saddle Points
[11] p S1.SS0.SSS0.Px2.p1.1 Stochastic gradient descent is the driving horse for solving large scale optimization in machine learning and deep learning. Gradient descent can be viewed as a steepest descent procedure that iteratively improves the solution by following the direction that maximally decreases the loss function within a small neighborhood of the previous solution. Specifically, for minimizing a loss function L ⁡ ( θ ) L(\theta) , each iteration of steepest descent updates the parameter via θ ← θ + ϵ ​ δ \theta\leftarrow\theta+\epsilon\delta , where ϵ \epsilon is a small step size and δ \delta is an update direction chosen to maximally decrease the loss L ⁡ ( θ + ϵ ​ δ ) L(\theta+\epsilon\delta) of the updated parameter under a norm constraint ‖ δ ‖ ≤ 1 \left\lVert\delta\right\rVert\leq 1 , where ‖ ⋅ ‖ \left\lVert\cdot\right\rVert denotes the Euclidean norm. When ∇ L ​ ( θ ) ≠ 0 \nabla L(\theta)\neq 0 and ϵ \epsilon is infinitesimal, the optimal descent direction δ \delta equals the negative gradient direction, that is, δ = − ∇ L ( θ ) / ‖ ∇ L ( θ ) ‖ \delta=-\nabla L(\theta)/\left\lVert\nabla L(\theta)\right\rVert , yielding a descent of L ⁡ ( θ + ϵ ​ δ ) − L ⁡ ( θ ) ≈ − ϵ ⁡ ‖ ∇ L ​ ( θ ) ‖ L(\theta+\epsilon\delta)-L(\theta)\approx-\epsilon\left\lVert\nabla L(\theta)\right\rVert . At a critical point with a zero gradient ( ∇ L ​ ( θ ) = 0 \nabla L(\theta)=0 ), the steepest descent direction depends on the spectrum of the Hessian matrix ∇ 2 L ​ ( θ ) \nabla^{2}L(\theta) . Denote by λ m ​ i ​ n \lambda_{min} the minimum eigenvalue of ∇ 2 L ​ ( θ ) \nabla^{2}L(\theta) and v m ​ i ​ n v_{min} its associated eigenvector. When λ m ​ i ​ n > 0 \lambda_{min}>0 , the point θ \theta is a stable local minimum and no further improvement can be made in the infinitesimal neighborhood. When λ m ​ i ​ n < 0 \lambda_{min}<0 , the point θ \theta is a saddle point or local maximum, and the steepest descent direction equals the eigenvector ± v m ​ i ​ n \pm v_{min} , which yields an ϵ 2 ​ λ m ​ i ​ n / 2 \epsilon^{2}\lambda_{min}/2 decrease on the loss. 1 1 1 The property of the case when λ m ​ i ​ n = 0 \lambda_{min}=0 depends on higher order information. In practice, it has been shown that there is no need to explicitly calculate the negative eigenvalue direction, because saddle points and local maxima are unstable and can be escaped by using gradient descent with random initialization or stochastic noise ( Lee et al., 2016 ; Jin et al., 2017 , e.g.,) .

[42] h3  2.2 Splitting Deep Neural Networks
[43] p S2.SS2.p1.1 In practice, we need to split multiple neurons simultaneously, which may be of different types, or locate in different layers of a deep neural network. The key questions are if the optimal splitting strategies of different neurons influence each other in some way, and how to compare the gain of splitting different neurons and select the best subset of neurons to split under a budget constraint.
[44] p S2.SS2.p2.1 It turns out the answers are simple. We show that the change of loss caused by splitting a set of neurons is simply the sum of the splitting terms I ​ I ​ ( 𝜹 , 𝒘 , θ ) I\!I(\boldsymbol{\delta},\boldsymbol{w};\theta) of the individual neurons. Therefore, we can calculate the splitting matrix of each neuron independently without considering the other neurons, and compare the “splitting desirability” of the different neurons by their minimum eigenvalues (splitting indexes). This motivates our main algorithm (Algorithm 1 ), in which we progressively split the neurons with the most negative splitting indexes following their own splitting gradients. Since the neurons can be in different layers and of different types, this provides an adaptive way to grow neural network structures to fit best with data.

[58] h4  Computational Efficiency
[59] p S2.SS2.SSS0.Px1.p1.1 The computational cost of exactly evaluating all the splitting indexes and gradients on a data instance is 𝒪 ⁡ ( n ​ d 3 ) \mathcal{O}(nd^{3}) , where n n is the number of neurons and d d is the number of the parameters of each neuron. Note that this is much better than evaluating the Hessian matrix, which costs 𝒪 ⁡ ( N 3 ) \mathcal{O}(N^{3}) , where N N is the total number of parameters (e.g., N ≥ n ​ d N\geq nd ). In practice, d d is not excessively large or can be controlled by identifying a subset of important neurons to split. Further computational speedup can be obtained by using efficient gradient-based large scale eigen-computation methods, which we investigate in future work.
[60] h3  2.3 Splitting as ∞ \infty -Wasserstein Steepest Descent
[61] p S2.SS3.p1.1 We present a functional aspect of our approach, in which we frame the co-optimization of the neural parameters and structures into a functional optimization in the space of distributions of the neuron weights, and show that our splitting strategy can be viewed as a second-order descent for escaping saddle points in the ∞ \infty -Wasserstein space of distributions, while the standard parametric gradient descent corresponds to a first-order descent in the same space.

[80] h4  Remark
[81] p S2.SS3.SSS0.Px1.p1.1 There has been a line of theoretical works on analyzing gradient-based learning of neural networks via 2 2 -Wasserstein gradient flow by considering the mean field limit when the number of neurons m m goes to infinite ( m → ∞ ) (m\to\infty) ( Mei et al., 2018 ; Chizat & Bach, 2018 , e.g.,) . These analysis focus on the first-order descent on the 2 2 -Wasserstein space as a theoretical tool for understanding the behavior of gradient descent on overparameterized neural networks. Our framework is significant different, since we mainly consider the second-order descent on the ∞ \infty -Wasserstein space, and the case of finite number of neurons m m in order to derive practical algorithms.

[224] h3  B.2 Learning Interpretable Neural Network
[225] p A2.SS2.p1.1 We provide more details of our experiments on learning the interpretable neural network in this section.
[226] h4  Setting
[227] p A2.SS2.SSS0.Px1.p1.1 We adopt the interpretable neural architecture proposed in Li et al. (2018) as our testbed. Unlike standard black box neural networks, this architecture contains a special prototype layer in the classifier, which includes a set of prototypes that are enforced to be similar to realistic images for increasing interpretability. In this model, the input image x x is first mapped to a lower-dimensional representation v v based on its distance with the prototypes ‖ θ − e ⁡ ( x ) ‖ \left\lVert\theta-e(x)\right\rVert , where θ \theta represents the parameter of a prototype and e ⁡ ( x ) e(x) is an encoder function. In addition, to ensure the prototypes are interpretable, Li et al. (2018) introduce a regularization term that minimizes the minimum square distance between the prototypes and the training data, which has a form of ‖ θ − e ⁡ ( x ) ‖ \left\lVert\theta-e(x)\right\rVert . Therefore, the overall training loss has a form of 𝔼 x ​ [ Φ ⁡ ( ‖ θ − e ⁡ ( x ) ‖ ) ] \mathbb{E}_{x}[\Phi(\left\lVert\theta-e(x)\right\rVert)] , for which our splitting method can be applied.
[228] p A2.SS2.SSS0.Px1.p2.1 For the experiment, we evaluate our method on the MNIST dataset. We feed each prototype into the decoder function to generate prototype visualization in the pixel space. We use the same encoder and decoder architectures, as suggested in Li et al. (2018) and refer the reader to Li et al. (2018) for more implementation details. We compare our splitting method with new initialization, random splitting, net2net method. Start from one prototype template, and all the methods split one template after 60 epochs training. New initialization method adds a new randomly initialized template each time, random splitting and net2net all randomly choose one template to split; the difference is that random splitting gives a shift vector after splitting while net2net method follows giving a larger learning rate in the new network. We also set a baseline that trains the model from scratch, which means directly train a fix number prototype templates.

[235] h3  B.3 Lightweight Neural Architectures for Image Classification
[236] p A2.SS3.p1.1 We investigate the effectiveness of our methods in learning novel compact deep neural networks for image classification.
[237] h4  Dataset
[238] p A2.SS3.SSS0.Px1.p1.1 We use the CIFAR-10 benchmark dataset. CIFAR-10 2 2 2 https://www.cs.toronto.edu/~kriz/cifar.html consists of images drawn from 10 classes. The training and test sets contain 50,000 and 10,000 images, respectively. We adopt a standard data argumentation scheme (mirroring and shifting) that is widely used for this dataset ( Liu et al., 2019b ; Liu et al., 2017 ) . The input images are normalized using channel means and standard derivations.
[239] h4  Networks
[240] p A2.SS3.SSS0.Px2.p1.1 We experiment with two popular deep neural architectures, MobileNet ( Howard et al., 2017 ) and VGG19 ( Simonyan & Zisserman, 2015 ) , as our testbed. MobileNet consists of 1 standard convolution layer that follows 13 depthwise and pointwise convolution layers; VGG19 consists of standard 16 convolution layers.
[241] h4  Training Settings
[242] p A2.SS3.SSS0.Px3.p1.1 We are interested in training structured compact networks and treat kernel weights as neurons. For example, given a convolution layer with n o ​ u ​ t × n i ​ n × k × k n_{out}\times n_{in}\times k\times k parameters, with n o ​ u ​ t n_{out} denotes the number of output channels and n i ​ n n_{in} represents the number of input channels and k k is the filter size. In this way, and each neuron has a size n i ​ n × k × k n_{in}\times k\times k and there are n o ​ u ​ t n_{out} neurons in total. We start with a relatively small size network and gradually grow the network by splitting the neurons with minimum eigenvalues follow Algorithm 1 . For MobileNet, we keep the first convolution layer as the same (=32) and set the number of depthwise and pointwise channels to be 16. For VGG19, we set the number of channels to 16 for all layers.
[243] p A2.SS3.SSS0.Px3.p2.1 We set the batch size to be 64. We use stochastic gradient descent with an initial learning rate 0.1 0.1 , the learning rate is divided by 10 at 50% and 75% of the total number of training epochs. We use a weight decay of 10 − 4 10^{-4} and a Nesterove momentum of 0.9 without dampening.
[244] p A2.SS3.SSS0.Px3.p3.1 After splitting, we obtain narrow deep neural networks, which are then retrained or finetuned. We use the same optimization settings as in standard training. We retrain (or finetune) all networks for 160 epochs.
[245] p A2.SS3.SSS0.Px3.p4.1 Note that our splitting matrix (see Eq. 7 ) involves the second order information of the activation function. In practice, we replace all ReLu activation with Softplus to prevent numerical issues in calculating the splitting matrices. We also apply this in other following experiments that contain ReLu activation function in the network.
[246] h4  Pruning
[247] p A2.SS3.SSS0.Px4.p1.1 To demonstrate the effectiveness of our method in learning lightweight deep neural networks. We compare with two structured pruning algorithms: Batch-normalization-based pruning (Bn-prune) ( Liu et al., 2017 ) : Bn-prune imposes L1-sparsity on channel-wise scaling factors from Batch Normalization layers during training, and prunes channels with lower scaling factors afterwards; and L1-based pruning (L1-prune) ( Li et al., 2017 ) : in each layer, a certain percentage of filters with small L1-norm weights will be pruned.
[248] p A2.SS3.SSS0.Px4.p2.1 For all pruning baselines, we use the implementation provided by Liu et al. (2019b) . We train all backbone networks from scratch as baselines. For Bn-prune, we set the sparsity term to be 0.0001 for all the cases.

[274] h3  C.2 Knowledge Transferring via Neural Splitting
[275] p A3.SS2.p1.1 In this section, we present a preliminary result that shows that our method can be used to accelerate the training for online learning and transfer learning by taking advantage of transferring knowledge between neurons via splitting. The idea is to show that as new data arrives in online learning or transfer learning, our method can be used as an efficient approach to train augmented models while leveraging the previous knowledge by splitting neurons from the previous trained models (see ( Chen et al., 2016 ) ).
[276] p A3.SS2.p2.1 We present empirical evaluations of our method and baseline methods on the MNIST dataset. For simplicity, we train an one layer auto-encoder with 10 hidden units. We set the batch size to be 256 and use stochastic gradient descent with 0.1 learning rate. To simulate an online setting, we proceed as follows: we first train the autoencoder to converge with 15% data points randomly sampled from the MNIST dataset. Then we widen the network by introducing two new hidden units and fine-tune the network with 30% newly observed MNIST subset using the same learning rate as before. For Random Split (Net2Net) , we grow the network by randomly selecting two hidden units and replicating all the corresponding weights. However, instead of perturbing the neurons, we double the learning rate following the setting suggested in ( Chen et al., 2016 ) . As shown in Figure 10 , our method gives faster convergence than the baseline approaches.
