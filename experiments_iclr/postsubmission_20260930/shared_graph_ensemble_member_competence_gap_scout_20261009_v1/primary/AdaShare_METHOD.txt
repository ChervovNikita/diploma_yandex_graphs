3 Proposed Method
Figure 2: Illustration of our proposed approach. AdaShare learns the layer sharing pattern among multiple tasks through predicting a select-or-skip policy decision sampled from the learned task-specific policy distribution (logits). These select-or-skip vectors define which blocks should be executed in different tasks. A block is said to be shared across two tasks if it is being used by both of them or task-specific if it is being used by only one task for predicting the output.
During training, both policy logits and network parameters are jointly learned using standard back-propagation through Gumbel-Softmax Sampling. We use task-specific losses and policy regularizations (to encourage sparsity and sharing) in training. Best viewed in color.
Given a set of [MATH K] tasks [MATH \bm{T}=\{\mathcal{T}_{1},\mathcal{T}_{2},\cdots,\mathcal{T}_{K}\}] defined over a dataset, our goal is to seek an adaptive feature sharing mechanism that decides what network layers should be shared across which tasks and what layers should be task-specific in order to improve the accuracy, while taking the resource efficiency into account for scalable multi-task learning.
Approach Overview.
Figure 2 illustrates an overview of our proposed approach. Generally, we seek a binary random variable [MATH \mathbf{u}_{l,k}] (a.k.a policy) for each layer [MATH l] and task [MATH \mathcal{T}_{k}] that determines whether the [MATH l] -th layer in a deep neural network is selected to execute or skipped when solving [MATH \mathcal{T}_{k}] to obtain the optimal sharing pattern, yielding the best overall performance over the task set [MATH T] .
Shortcut connections are widely used in recent network architectures (e.g. ResNet [18], ResNeXt [64], and DenseNet [21]) and achieve strong performance in many recognition tasks.
These connections make these architectures resilient to removal of layers [59], which benefits our method. In this paper, we consider using ResNets [18] with [MATH L] residual blocks.
In particular, a residual block is said to be shared across two tasks if it is being used by both of them, or task-specific if it is being used by only one task for predicting the output. In this way, the select-or-skip policy of all blocks and tasks ( [MATH \mathbf{U}=\{\mathbf{u}_{l,k}\}_{l\leq L,k\leq K}] ) determines the adaptive feature sharing mechanism over the given task set [MATH T] .
As the number of potential configurations for [MATH \mathbf{U}] is [MATH 2^{L\times K}] which grows exponentially with the number of blocks and tasks, it becomes intractable to manually find such a [MATH \mathbf{U}] to get the optimal feature sharing pattern in multi-task learning. Instead of handcrafting this policy,
we adopt Gumbel-Softmax Sampling [25] to optimize [MATH \mathbf{U}] jointly with the network parameters [MATH W] through standard back-propagation. Moreover, we introduce two policy regularizations to achieve effective knowledge sharing in a compact multi-task network, as well as a curriculum learning strategy to stabilize the optimization in the early stages.
After the training finishes, we sample the binary decision [MATH u_{l,k}] for each block [MATH l] from [MATH \mathbf{u}_{l,k}] to decide what blocks to select or skip in the task [MATH \mathcal{T}_{k}] . Specifically, with the help of the select-or-skip decisions, we form a novel and non-trivial network architecture for MTL parameter-sharing, and share knowledge at different levels across all tasks in a flexible and efficient way. At test time, when a novel input is presented to the multi-task network, the optimal policy is followed, selectively choosing what blocks to compute for each task.
Our proposed approach not only encourages positive sharing among tasks via shared blocks but also minimizes negative interference by using task-specific blocks when necessary.
Learning a Task-Specific Policy.
In AdaShare, we learn the select-or-skip policy [MATH \mathbf{U}] and network weights [MATH W] jointly through standard back-propagation from our designed loss functions.
However, each select-or-skip policy [MATH \mathbf{u}_{l,k}] is discrete and non-differentiable and
this makes direct optimization difficult. Therefore, we adopt Gumbel-Softmax Sampling [25] to resolve this non-differentiability and enable direct optimization of the discrete policy [MATH \mathbf{u}_{l,k}] using back-propagation.
Gumbel-Softmax Sampling. The Gumbel-Softmax trick [25, 36]
is a simple and effective way to substitutes the original non-differentiable sample from a discrete distribution with a differentiable sample from a corresponding Gumbel-Softmax distribution.
We let [MATH \pi_{l,k}=[1-\alpha_{l,k},\alpha_{l,k}]] be the distribution vector of the binary random variable [MATH \mathbf{u}_{l,k}] that we want to optimize, where the logit [MATH \alpha_{l,k}] represents the probability that the [MATH l] -th block is selected to execute in the task [MATH \mathcal{T}_{k}] .
In Gumbel-Softmax Sampling, instead of directly sampling a select-or-skip decision [MATH u_{l,k}] for the [MATH l] -th block in the task [MATH \mathcal{T}_{k}] from its distribution [MATH \pi_{l,k}] , we generate it as,
[MATH \displaystyle u_{l,k}=\argmax_{j\in\{0,1\}}\big(\log\pi_{l,k}(j)+G_{l,k}(j)\big),~]
(1)
where [MATH G_{l,k}=-\log(-\log U_{l,k})] is a standard Gumbel distribution with [MATH U_{l,k}] sampled from a uniform i.i.d. distribution [MATH \text{Unif}(0,1)] .
To remove the non-differentiable argmax operation in Eq. 1, the Gumbel Softmax trick relaxes [MATH \text{one-hot}(u_{l,k})\in\{0,1\}^{2}] (the one-hot encoding of [MATH u_{l,k}] ) to [MATH v_{l,k}\in\mathbb{R}^{2}] (the soft select-or-skip decision for the [MATH l] -th block in [MATH \mathcal{T}_{k}] ) with the reparameterization trick [25]:
[MATH \displaystyle v_{l,k}(j)=\frac{\exp\big((\log\pi_{l,k}(j)+G_{l,k}(j))/\tau\big)}{\sum\limits_{i\in\{0,1\}}\exp\big((\log\pi_{l,k}(i)+G_{l,k}(i))/\tau\big)},~]
(2)
where [MATH j\in\{0,1\}] and [MATH \tau] is the temperature of the softmax. Clearly, when [MATH \tau>0] , the Gumbel-Softmax distribution [MATH p_{\tau}(v_{l,k})] is smooth so [MATH \pi_{l,k}] (or [MATH \alpha_{l,k}] ) can be directly optimized by gradient descent, and when [MATH \tau] approaches 0, the soft decision [MATH v_{l,k}] becomes the same as [MATH \text{one-hot}(u_{l,k})] and the corresponding Gumbel-Softmax distribution [MATH p_{\tau}(v_{l,k})] becomes identical to the discrete distribution [MATH \pi_{l,k}] .
Following [17, 61], we optimize the discrete policy [MATH \mathbf{u}_{l,k},\forall\ l\leq L,k\leq K] at once. During the training, we use the soft task-specific decision [MATH v_{l,k}] given by Eq. 2 in both forward and backward passes [61].
Also, we set [MATH \tau=5] as the initial value and gradually anneal it down to 0 during the training, as in [17, 61].
After the learning of the policy distribution, we obtain the discrete task-specific decision [MATH U] by sampling from the learned policy distribution [MATH p(\mathbf{U})] .
Loss Functions.
Task-specific losses only optimizes for accuracy without taking efficiency into account.
However, we prefer to form a compact sub-model for each single task, in which blocks are omitted as much as possible without deteriorating the prediction accuracy.
To this end, we propose a sparsity regularization [MATH \mathcal{L}_{sparsity}] to enhance the model’s compactness by minimizing the log-likelihood of the probability of a block being executed as
[MATH \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{sparsity}=\sum\limits_{l\leq L,k\leq K}\log\alpha_{l,k}.]
(3)
Furthermore, we introduce a loss [MATH \mathcal{L}_{sharing}] that encourages residual block sharing across tasks to avoid the whole network being split up by tasks with little knowledge shared among them. Encouraging
sharing reduces the redundancy of knowledge separately kept in task-specific blocks of related tasks and results in an more efficient sharing scheme that better utilizes residual blocks.
Specifically, we minimize the weighted sum of [MATH L_{1}] distances between the policy logits of different tasks with an emphasis on encouraging the sharing of bottom blocks which contain low-level knowledge.
More formally, we define [MATH \mathcal{L}_{sharing}] as
[MATH \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{sharing}=\sum\limits_{k_{1},k_{2}\leq K}\sum\limits_{l\leq L}\frac{L-l}{L}|\alpha_{l,k_{1}}-\alpha_{l,k_{2}}|.]
(4)
Finally, the overall loss [MATH \mathcal{L}] is defined as
[MATH \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{total}=\sum\limits_{k}\lambda_{k}\mathcal{L}_{k}+\lambda_{sp}\mathcal{L}_{sparsity}+\lambda_{sh}\mathcal{L}_{sharing},]
(5)
where [MATH \mathcal{L}_{k}] represent the task-specific losses with task weightings [MATH \lambda_{k}] . [MATH \lambda_{sp}] and [MATH \lambda_{sh}] are the balance parameters for [MATH \mathcal{L}_{sparsity}] and [MATH \mathcal{L}_{sharing}] respectively. The
additional losses push the policy learning to automatically induce resource efficiency while preserving the recognition accuracy of different tasks.
Training Strategy.
Following [61, 65], we optimize over the network weights and policy distribution parameters alternately on separate training splits.
To encourage the better convergence, we “warm up” the network weights by sharing all blocks across tasks (i.e., hard-parameter sharing) for a few epochs to provide a good starting point for the policy learning. Furthermore, instead of optimizing over the whole decision space in the early training stage, we develop a simple yet effective strategy to gradually enlarge the decision space and form a set of learning tasks from easy to hard, inspired by curriculum learning [4]. Specifically, for the [MATH l] -th ( [MATH l<L] ) epoch, we only learn the policy distribution of last [MATH l] blocks. We then gradually learn the distribution parameters of additional blocks as [MATH l] increases and learn the joint distribution for all blocks after [MATH L] epochs.
After the policy distribution parameters get fully trained, we sample a select-or-skip decision, i.e., feature sharing pattern, from the best policy to form a new network and optimize using the full training set.
Parameter Complexity.
Note that unlike [8, 17], we optimize over the logits [MATH A={\alpha_{l,k}}_{l\leq L,k\leq K}] for the overall select-or-skip policy [MATH \mathbf{U}] directly instead of learning a policy network from the semantic task embedding or an image input. As a result, besides the original network, we only occupy [MATH L] additional parameters for any new task, which results in a negligible parameter count increase over the total number of network parameters.
Our model has also a significantly lower number of parameters (about 50% lower while learning two tasks) compared to the recent deep multi-task learning methods [16, 33]. Therefore, in terms of memory, our model scales very well with more tasks learned together.
