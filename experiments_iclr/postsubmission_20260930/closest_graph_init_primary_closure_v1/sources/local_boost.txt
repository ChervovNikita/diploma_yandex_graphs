Local Learning with Boosting-based Backpropagation-Free 
Graph Neural Networks
Luca Pasa1,2 · Paolo Frazzetto1 · Nicolò Navarin1,2 · Alessandro Sperduti1,2,3
Machine Learning (2026) 115:111
https://doi.org/10.1007/s10994-026-07041-x
Abstract
The framework of Backpropagation-Free Graph Neural Networks (BF-GNNs) enables lo -
cal learning at the neuron level in GNNs. While BF-GNNs can match the performance of 
their backpropagation-based counterparts, they may develop redundant internal representa-
tions that limit further gains. To address this issue, we propose an innovative architecture 
dubbed Boosting-based Backpropagation-Free GNN (B 3F-GNN), where each network 
module contains multiple backpropagation-free neurons trained locally and combined as 
a classifier. Within each layer, later modules exploit error signals from earlier trained 
modules to refine predictions by diversifying internal representations. We implement this 
approach with two complementary boosting strategies: sample reweighting, in the spirit of 
AdaBoost, and error-guided prototype selection for gating, which concentrates non-linear -
ities where previous modules struggled. The modular design also enables any-time incre -
mental training by adding more modules on demand within resource constraints. An abla -
tion study and in-depth experimental analysis show that both strategies reduce redundancy 
and increase specialization, leading to statistically significant accuracy improvements over 
backpropagation-based counterparts on standard node-classification benchmarks.

1 Introduction
It is widely acknowledged that standard backpropagation is not considered a biologically 
plausible learning mechanism (Baldi, 2021); in fact, evidence from neuroscience suggests 
biological learning processes are local and potentially exploit feedback mechanisms (Pearl 
et al., 2024). While biological plausibility is not a necessary requirement for effective arti -
ficial learning systems, insights from biological learning can nevertheless inspire alterna -
tive computational paradigms that emphasize locality, modularity, and adaptability. Local

Machine Learning (2026) 115:111
learning mechanisms naturally favor modular organization, which can improve learning 
efficiency and flexibility. In such settings, performance can be enhanced by recruiting 
additional computational modules, while previously trained components may be reused or 
adapted for novel tasks. Thus, the development of local learning techniques can enable the 
implementation of learning strategies to efficiently and effectively cope with dynamical and 
complex environments, where events are related spatially and temporally. In this context, 
the development of local and modular learning techniques represents a promising direc -
tion for designing learning systems that can efficiently handle structured, relational data. 
Traditional machine learning approaches that rely on fixed-size vector representations may 
struggle to fully exploit such structure, motivating the exploration of models, such as graph-
based architectures, that are better aligned with the relational nature of many real-world 
problems (Papamarkou et al., 2024).
In the last few years, many researchers within the machine learning community have 
explored the field of Geometric Deep Learning (Bronstein et al., 2021), which seeks to 
generalize deep learning techniques to non-Euclidean domains, with Graph Neural Net -
works representing a central pillar in the field. Learning structured representations is further 
motivated by insights from neuroscience, providing evidence of the presence of structured 
information storage in the brain (Ormrod, 2020), exemplified by cognitive maps for the 
representation of spatial relationships (Grieves & Jeffery, 2017) or the topological structures 
found in neural manifolds (Chaudhuri et al., 2019; Gardner et al., 2022).
An example of a proposal in the literature that aims to introduce a local learning approach 
jointly with the treatment of sets of correlated events is represented by Backpropagation-
Free Graph Convolutional Networks (BF-GCNs), which are neural models for graph data 
that do not rely on backpropagation for training (Pasa et al., 2022). BF-GCNs are based 
on Gated Linear Networks (see Section 2.4), that are biologically plausible (Sezener et 
al., 2022) piecewise linear models where the non-linearity depends on a gating mecha -
nism based on sets of prototypes that are fixed during training. BF-neurons in BF-GCNs 
extend such a gating mechanism to graph convolution filters, selecting a weight vector to 
process a node’s attributes based on its context. The goal of BF-GCNs is to define neural 
networks for graph processing without using backpropagation for training. This approach 
aims to enhance biological plausibility, improve sample efficiency, and simplify the training 
process. On the one hand, since each neuron is trained independently based solely on its 
inputs and the target value, the training process is highly parallelizable. On the other hand, 
the local nature of training can lead to an overall worse representation compared to global 
training using backpropagation. In particular, different neurons can exhibit strongly corre -
lated responses, resulting in redundant information in the learned representation. This issue 
is even more pronounced in graph models that exploit graph convolutional operators than 
in networks with vectorial inputs, since the use of convolutional or more generally invari -
ant operators applied at the node level tends to limit the variance between the computed 
node embeddings. A possible solution to the representation redundancy problem is to design 
mechanisms that encourage differentiation among neurons.
In this paper, we aim to maintain the local training nature of BF-GCNs while explor -
ing diversification mechanisms based on Boosting (Freund & Schapire, 1995), where we 
consider groups of neurons, i.e., Boosting Units (BUs), in the same layer. In the resulting 
architecture, referred to as the Boosting-based Backpropagation-Free GNN (B3F-GNN), the 
neurons within each BU are trained independently and in parallel. Each BU is connected to

the next one in a cascading manner. The subsequent BU receives input information about 
the examples misclassified by the preceding BU. This information enables the BU to focus 
primarily on those examples, thereby differentiating it from the preceding one. We consider 
two ways to incorporate error information: (i) weighting the input examples based on the 
loss reported by the preceding BU, similarly to the classic AdaBoost scheme (Freund & 
Schapire, 1995); (ii) guiding the prototype selection in the neurons of a BU with the infor -
mation about the loss reported by the preceding BU for the input examples. Both methods 
are developed with the aim of encouraging diversification among neurons in different BUs.
Overall, the B3F-GNN architecture allows for training the neurons within the same BU in 
parallel, while neurons in different BUs exploit and correct the errors of their predecessors. 
This aspect, combined with the efficiency and locality of training, opens up the possibility 
of training a model that increases in complexity over time, making it particularly suitable 
for scenarios where the available computational resources are limited. In such settings, the 
model’s in-time nature enables it to start with a small number of BUs (even one) and pro -
gressively expand it by adding new units based on the desired level of error, in an incremen-
tal fashion, implementing an any-time training procedure in practice.
The experimental analysis and ablation study reported in the paper demonstrate that both 
proposed boosting mechanisms enhance predictive performance compared to the origi -
nal BF-GCN models and their backpropagation-based variants. In particular, the results 
obtained by B 3F-GNN show a statistically significant improvement in accuracy over the 
backpropagation-based counterparts. This indicates that adopting local and modular learn -
ing strategies, which allow for more efficient use of computational resources, can offer prac-
tical benefits. Furthermore, in the ablation study, we evaluate the impact of incorporating 
the proposed boosting methodologies. In particular, we analyze the effect of the proposed 
techniques in differentiating the neurons, highlighting two main effects: (i) the proposed 
method successfully decreases the correlation between BUs; (ii) the BUs in the model tend 
to act as correctors, progressively mitigating the errors made by the previous ones.
In summary, the main contributions of the paper are:
 ● a novel backpropagation-free modular neural architecture for learning on graphs in an 
incremental any-time fashion;
 ● the introduction of two novel boosting approaches designed to overcome the limitations 
of the BF-GNN architecture;
 ● an in-depth analysis of how boosting strategies impact neuron diversification and influ-
ence the model selection process;
 ● an empirical analysis showing that the incremental recruitment of additional BU mod -
ules improves performance;
 ● an extensive experimental assessment of the predictive performance of B 3F-GNN, 
showing a statistically significant improvement over a backpropagation-based version 
of the same architecture.

2.3 Gated Linear Networks
Backpropagation-free methods have attracted growing interest as viable alternatives to 
conventional gradient-based optimization techniques. These methods provide advantages 
such as enhanced parallelism and biological plausibility. Among the various approaches, 
Gated Linear Networks (GLNs) (Veness et al., 2021) stand out as a particularly promising 
methodology, both in terms of predictive performance as well as biological plausibility 
(especially with a recently proposed variation (Sezener et al., 2022)). GLNs are designed 
as models composed of neurons that can be trained locally and independently using only 
task supervision. Their key principle is that, with an appropriate choice of loss function, the 
local optimization problem for each neuron becomes convex. More precisely, each neuron 
is trained to predict the target value and can be optimized independently of the rest of the 
network, given its input. Each neuron is defined as a Gated Geometric Mixer: an ensemble 
technique that assigns weights to weak predictors in its input. Formally, given an input vec-

3 B 3F-GNN
This study presents an innovative local learning-oriented architecture for graph data that 
exploits BF-conv as a building block. The methodology employs a boosting-based mecha -
nism designed to implement an error correction strategy through the use of multiple indepen-
dently trained neurons. It also promotes specialization among neurons, thereby improving 
their capacity to identify diverse data patterns. The architecture, dubbed B3F-GNN, is com-
posed of several distinct components. First, the neurons are organized into layers, similar 
to the architecture proposed in Pasa et al. ( 2022). Each layer consists of a set of Boosting 
Units (BUs), where each unit comprises a group of neurons that can be trained in parallel 
and function as a committee to produce a single output. BF-convs are designed to efficiently 
perform binary classification tasks. Consequently, in multiclass settings, each BU is com -
posed of neurons dedicated to a specific one-vs-rest sub-task, effectively acting as class-spe-
cialized detectors. Consequently, each layer contains distinct sets of neurons specialized in 
their respective sub-tasks, each with its own tailored boosting mechanism. BUs serve as the 
fundamental building blocks of our boosting methodology. Each unit propagates informa -
tion to the next BU in the same layer regarding the quality of predictions for each input. This 
information is then leveraged to assign larger weights to misclassified samples, refining the 
learning process and enhancing prototype selection to help specialize the behaviors of each 
BU. Figure 1 presents a schematic representation of the overall architecture of B3F-GNN.
In the following, we formally define the Boosting Units and the two boosting strategies 
we adopted. The first strategy is reminiscent of the AdaBoost algorithm, which is commonly

The other, more innovative strategy we developed focuses on the gating mechanism

4 Experimental Results
We evaluated the proposed Boosting-based BF-GNN (B3F-GNN) on four benchmark data-
sets for graph node classification—Citeseer, Cora, Pubmed, and WikiCS (Mernyei & Can-
gea, 2020)—where nodes are assigned to predefined topic categories. Citeseer, Cora, and 
Pubmed are citation networks with nodes representing research papers and edges indicating 
citation links, using 0/1-valued bag-of-words feature vectors. WikiCS comprises Wikipedia 
articles on Computer Science, with nodes as articles and edges as hyperlinks, and node 
features derived from pre-trained word embeddings. The datasets followed a standard data 
split: 60% for training, 20% for validation, and 20% for testing, with classification accuracy 
used as the performance metric.
All models are implemented using PyG (Fey & Lenssen, 2019). The execution of the 
experiments was performed with NVIDIA RTX A5000 24GB GPUs. As baseline meth -
ods, we considered a standard backpropagation-based GNN architecture that utilizes GCN 
and GraphConv convolutions, along with the backpropagation-free architecture introduced 
in Pasa et al. (2022). In line with this approach, we also conducted experiments using both 
node labels X and hidden representations H as context Z for the input data. The results were 
averaged by performing 5 runs for each model. For more details, please check the publicly 
available code.1 Differently from many papers in the literature, we paid particular attention 
to ensuring a fair model selection process to obtain an unbiased estimate of the true error, as 
recommended in Errica et al. ( 2020). Thus, we applied the same rigorous model selection 
procedure across all experiments, selecting the hyperparameters of each method based on 
performance on the validation set, optimized using a grid search. Preliminary experiments

4.1 Results Discussion
Table 2 presents a comparison of classification accuracy between Backpropagation-based 
and Backpropagation-Free models proposed in Pasa et al. (2022), along with the proposed 
B3F-GNN. The results show that B 3F-GNN achieves the best performance across all the 
considered datasets, demonstrating the benefits of adopting boosting strategies. Notably, 
these strategies are feasible because we rely on the BF framework, which enables us to treat 
each BU as an individual classifier. Even when comparing models that use the same type of 
convolution (GCN and GraphConv), the results suggest that the proposed approach consis-
tently achieves performance that is either higher or at least comparable to the other consid-
ered methods. It is also worth noting that B3F-GNN surpasses the accuracy achieved by its 
backpropagation-based counterpart, proving that by leveraging the advantages of a locally 
trained BF model, it is possible to attain even higher performance than with standard back-
propagation. Additionally, since B 3F-GNN follows a backpropagation-free approach, the 
training of neurons within a BU can be performed independently, enabling parallelization.

4.2 Statistical Significance
To rigorously evaluate the statistical significance of observed performance improvements, 
moving beyond mere numerical comparisons of accuracy means, we conducted compre -
hensive statistical hypothesis testing. For each dataset and model, we considered all the 
test accuracies from the 5 dataset splits of the best-validated model configurations. This 
approach enabled a robust comparison of multiple classifiers on multiple dataset instances, 
ensuring enough statistical power (Demsar, 2006). Initially, the Friedman test was applied: 
it ranks model accuracies, grouped per dataset run, and tests the null hypothesis that all 
models are equivalent. For both GCN and GraphConv model families, the Friedman test 
yielded a p-value of ≤ 0.001 , leading to the rejection of the null hypothesis and corrobo -
rating a post-hoc analysis. Following the Friedman test, we performed Conover’s post-hoc 
test (Demsar, 2006), visually represented through Critical Difference (CD) diagrams, as 
shown in Figure 2. In these diagrams, models are positioned on the x-axis according to their 
average percentile performance (where a higher value indicates better performance, being 
first in the percentile rankings), and horizontal lines connect groups of models whose per -
formance is not statistically significantly different at a p ≤ 0.05 level.
For the GCN model family, the results clearly demonstrate that our proposed B3F-GCN 
models form a distinct, superior group. Despite the difference in their average numerical 
performance, the Conover’s test indicates no statistically significant difference between 
using X or H as context. This suggests that both context choices for B3F-GCN yield compa-
rable statistically significant improvements relative to other models. In contrast, the baseline 
GCN and the BF-GCN variants comprise a lower-performing group, within which no sig -
nificant differences across dataset runs were observed. More importantly, the B 3F-GCN-H 
model is statistically significantly superior to the GCN baseline and the BF-GCN models.
Similarly, for the GraphConv model family, the B 3F-GraphConv architectures exhibit 
significantly higher performance. The B3F-GraphConv-X model, with an average accuracy 
of 0.81, the B 3F-GraphConv-H, at 0.67, and the BF-GraphConv-H at 0.50, are grouped 
together, indicating no statistically significant difference between them. However, the B 3
F-GraphConv-X model is meaningfully better than the remaining models, including the 
GraphConv baseline.
These tests and the CD diagrams highlight the benefits of the B3F models across different 
datasets and architectures, given the consistent and statistically significant improvements 
over both baselines and BF variants.

5.3 Boosting Units Correlations
To better understand the mechanism behind the performance gains of our boosting approach, 
we conducted a comprehensive correlation analysis of the BU outputs in both a boosted B3F-
GNN and an equivalent non-boosted architecture. We focus our analysis on the Pubmed data-
set, where prior ablation studies indicated greater performance improvements, and notably, 
both of the validated versions comprised 16 units, guaranteeing a fair comparison between 
the two models. For the samples in the test set, we assessed that the unnormalized logits of 
each BU are normally distributed, and then we computed all the pairwise Pearson correlation 
coefficients. This procedure was repeated for each class. Finally, their values were averaged 
across all units, giving a mean correlation of ρ B =0 .59± 0.15 and ρ noB =0 .98± 0.01 
for the boosted and non-boosted version, respectively. Figure 4 further presents the correla-
tions across units: while the non-boosted model yields strongly correlated BU outputs (and 
thus a redundant representation), the B3F-GNN shows that different units are less correlated 
(i.e., more diverse one from each other), thus learning a less redundant representation. This 
analysis reveals that the boosting-based B3F-GNN architecture produces significantly more 
diversified classifier outputs compared to the non-boosted model. As expected, boosting not 
only improves overall performance, but it does so by encouraging greater diversity among 
units, thereby enhancing the model’s (or ensemble’s) ability to generalize and reducing the 
risk of redundant or overly similar learned representations.
5.4 Impact of Boosting in B3F-GNN applicability
One of the main advantages of Backpropagation-free training is the high parallelizability 
that can be achieved during both the training and regression phases (Pasa et al., 2024). Com-
pared to BF-GNNs, in B 3F-GNN parallelization can be applied to the neurons within the 
same BU, while neurons in different BUs have to be trained sequentially. However, achiev-
ing the highest possible level of parallelization in the original BF-GNN model requires 
running a very large number of parallel processes. While it is possible using powerful hard-
ware, we argue that the generally smaller model size of B 3F-GNN can provide a greater 
benefit, even at the expense of slightly lower parallelizability, especially in applications 
involving devices with limited computational and memory resources, where achieving the 
1 3
111 Page 14 of 20

maximum parallelism of BF-GNNs is not possible, while the computational benefits arising 
from using smaller models are tangible.
Moreover, the architecture introduced in B3F-GNN offers advantages beyond improved 
performance compared to its backpropagation-based counterparts. One particularly interest-
ing feature is the ability to dynamically add BUs. This is possible since each BU undergoes 
an independent training phase from the other ones in the same layer. Another crucial fac -
tor is that each BU is evaluated by computing error metrics independently during train -
ing, and this computation can be performed without depending on the state of neighboring 
BUs. These two aspects make it possible to increase the number of BUs on the fly without 
requiring model retraining. This is especially relevant in continual learning scenarios or in 
any application where savings resources as much as possible becomes a critical factor for 
success. From this perspective, the analysis presented in Figure 3 highlights the potential 
advantages of using boosting also within a hypothetical any-time incremental model con -
struction approach. Indeed, the models are tested by incrementally increasing the number 
of BUs, and the B 3F-GNN with a boosting strategy demonstrates a significant ability to 
achieve a high level of performance while using fewer BUs compared to its counterpart 
without the boosting mechanism.

The application of boosting techniques in GNNs that rely on backpropagation for train -
ing has been explored in the literature. Typically, the proposed architectures focus on 
specific tasks or types of input. Shi et al. ( 2021) proposed a novel method that combines 
ensemble learning with GNNs to address the issue of imbalanced datasets in node classifica-
tion. The AdaBoost algorithm enables the sequential training of multiple GNN classifiers 
by reweighting training samples according to the performance of earlier classifiers. This 
approach assigns greater weights to misclassified samples, directing successive classifiers’ 
focus to challenging cases. Moreover, transfer learning is utilized to expedite training by 
transferring learned parameters from earlier GNNs to later ones.
Another application of boosting in defining a GNN architecture is proposed in Ivanov 
and Prokhorenkova (2021) where the authors introduced a novel architecture called Boost-
GNN that combines Gradient Boosted Decision Trees (GBDT) with GNNs for learning 
on graphs with tabular node features. The key idea is to leverage GBDT to handle hetero -
geneous features and GNN to account for graph structure. Boosting is used to iteratively 
update the GBDT model by adding new trees that approximate the GNN loss function. 
This allows for end-to-end training, where the GBDT component effectively learns to fit 
the gradient updates of the GNN, enabling the backpropagation of the error signal from the 
network’s topology to the GBDT.
Guo et al. (2023) introduced Boosting-GNN, a technique that enhances a student GNN 
using knowledge distillation from multiple teacher GNNs of equal capacity. This is achieved 
through a sequential training strategy, allowing the student network to learn diverse knowl-
edge from each teacher individually. The approach integrates an adaptive temperature 
module to modulate the soft labels based on the teacher’s confidence, facilitating effective 
knowledge transfer, and a weight boosting mechanism that increases the focus on nodes 
misclassified by the teachers, thereby improving the student GNN’s learning.
Besides boosting, the idea of local learning, specializing a subset of nodes, is also 
exploited in Patel et al. ( 2023), where the authors proposed a method for local learning 
in neural networks using groups of neurons with local objective functions, allowing for 
parallel learning among model components. The approach involves splitting layers into 
sub-components, creating width-wise modularity in addition to depth-wise modularity. 
Additionally, the authors explored how to encourage these decoupled networks to learn 
diverse behaviors through forward communication. Both the approach proposed in Patel et 
al. (2023) and the one presented in this paper rely on dividing layers into subsets of neurons. 
However, the approach in Patel et al. ( 2023) does not consider structured data. Moreover, 
the two training methodologies significantly differ. In the proposed B3F-GNN, neurons are 
trained independently since each of them is a classifier, with training serialized only to share 
boosting parameters. In contrast, Patel et al. ( 2023) considers each group of neurons as a 
classifier. This distinction also affects how neuron diversity is enforced. In the present work, 
boosting serves as a mechanism to enhance diversity among the BUs, rather than in Patel 
et al. (2023), where a diversity-promoting term is inserted in the loss function to encourage 
diversity among classifiers.

7 Conclusion and Future Directions
In this paper, we introduced a novel backpropagation-free architecture for learning in the 
graph domain, dubbed B 3F-GNN, which leverages the BF framework and boosting tech -
niques to develop a local learning approach capable of achieving improved predictive per -
formance. In particular, we introduced a novel organization of the BF-Neuron that relies 
on the concept of Boosting Units, along with two ad-hoc boosting techniques designed to 
encourage specialization among groups of neurons. The two boosting techniques leverage 
different aspects of the BF-Neuron mechanism. The first technique operates on the weights 
of the various input samples and is reminiscent of AdaBoost-like methods. It adjusts the 
importance of each sample based on its contribution to the errors made by the previous BUs. 
The second technique focuses on the selection of the BF-Neuron prototypes, dynamically 
adjusting them based on the errors made by the preceding Boosting Unit, thereby refining 
the model’s representation and improving its performance. The resulting architecture also 
enables the definition of a model whose complexity can grow incrementally, starting the 
training with a limited number of BUs and progressively expanding it by adding new BUs 
based on the desired performance, making B3F-GNN particularly well-suited for resource-
constrained scenarios.
We evaluated the B3F-GNN on four node classification datasets. The results show that the 
proposed mechanism achieves improved accuracy compared to both backpropagation based 
counterparts and previously proposed BF-GNN models. Although the numerical differences 
in accuracy may appear limited, statistical significance tests confirm that the improvements 
obtained by B3F-GCN are statistically significant when compared with existing BF-GCNs 
in the literature as well as with backpropagation based approaches. In addition, for the 
BF-GraphConv variant, the achieved performance shows a statistically significant improve-
ment over the backpropagation based GraphConv operator. Furthermore, we analyzed how 
the proposed boosting techniques influence performance and neuron specialization, high -
lighting how the use of boosting strategies encourages differentiating the neurons, avoiding 
redundant information in the learned representations. We also observed that the later BUs 
specialize in correcting the errors made by the earlier ones, thereby enhancing the model’s 
overall accuracy.
As future work, we will explore a version of BUs capable of handling multiple classes 
simultaneously without relying on the one-vs-rest approach. This enhancement will help 
reduce the number of required BUs in the model, further decreasing the computational 
demand of this already lightweight approach. Finally, although the impact of the proposed 
boosting strategies is expected to be more prominent in graph based approaches, we plan to 
investigate their effectiveness in standard Gated Neural Networks.
