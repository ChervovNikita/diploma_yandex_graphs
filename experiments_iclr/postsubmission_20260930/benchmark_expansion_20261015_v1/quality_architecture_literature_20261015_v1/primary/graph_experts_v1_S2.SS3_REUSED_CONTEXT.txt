2.3. Methodology
Datasets.
We benchmark 14 real-world graphs that span various homophily levels and scales. Tiny graphs (with <\!1 k nodes) and synthetic-edge graphs are excluded for robustness (Platonov et al., 2023). See Appendix B.1 for the full dataset list.
Expert Architectures. Our study focuses on message-passing GNN architectures, where we systematically evaluate the effects of different modular components by varying one at a time within a unified framework (see Appendix B.2 for details).
Evaluation.
We evaluate the effectiveness of expert diversification techniques for node classification from three perspectives: Diversity, Complementarity, and Ensemble/MoE Accuracy. Each aspect captures a distinct property necessary for assessing the true benefits of expert diversification.
①Diversity:
Diversity among experts is a key factor driving performance gains in MoE frameworks. Let \mathcal{D}=\{(x_{n},y_{n})\}_{n=1}^{N} denote the evaluation set, and \{\mathcal{M}^{k}\}_{k=1}^{K} the set of K trained experts. The correctness indicator for expert k on sample n is defined as
c_{k,n}=\mathbbm{1}\{\mathcal{M}^{k}(x_{n})=y_{n}\}\in\{0,1\} ,
where \mathbbm{1}\{\cdot\} is the indicator function.
To quantify how differently the experts behave, we use Error Inconsistency (EI) (Gontijo-Lopes et al., 2022), which, for a pair of experts (i,j) , measures the probability that exactly one of them predicts correctly:
(1)
\mathrm{EI}_{i,j}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}|c_{i,n}-c_{j,n}|=\Pr(c_{i}\neq c_{j}).
For K\geq 2 , we generalize EI as the proportion of nodes not classified consistently—whether correctly or incorrectly—by all experts:
(2)
\mathrm{EI}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\min_{k}c_{k,n}=0\;\land\;\max_{k}c_{k,n}=1\right\},
where the indicator is 1 if there is at least one correct and one incorrect prediction among the experts for sample n .
②Complementarity:
Beyond diversity, we seek experts whose errors are non-overlapping, enabling complementary strengths. We first define the Upper-bound Accuracy as the proportion of nodes correctly predicted by at least one expert:
(3)
\mathrm{Acc}_{\text{oracle}}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathbbm{1}\left\{\max_{k}c_{k,n}=1\right\}.
We then define Complementary Gain (CG) as the improvement of this upper bound over the best single expert’s accuracy. Letting \mathrm{Acc}_{k} denote the accuracy of expert k ,
(4)
\mathrm{CG}=\mathrm{Acc}_{\text{oracle}}-\max_{k}\mathrm{Acc}_{k}.
These metrics reflect the performance of oracle ensembling, sidestepping the limitations of potentially suboptimal practical ensembles.
③Ensemble and MoE Accuracy:
We further assess the effectiveness of expert diversification using two practical ensemble approaches. (1) Global-Ensemble (denoted as Ensemble in the following) applies a global scalar weight to each expert for all nodes, with weights tuned on the validation set using the TPESampler from Optuna (Akiba et al., 2019). (2) Moscat Gating Ensemble (denoted as MoE) employs a gating model that assigns node-adaptive weights to each expert, dynamically integrating their predictions for each node based on validation performance. We evaluate the practical ensemble effectiveness of expert diversification using these two methods.
