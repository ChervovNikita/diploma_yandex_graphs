Abstract 
Deep ensemble learning, in which multiple models are 
combined, is widely used to improve the generalization 
performance of deep learning models. To maximize the 
effect of ensemble learning, “Diversity,” in which each 
model has a different output distribution, is important, and 
both ensuring diversity and improving accuracy are required. 
In this study, we propose a new method called “Dynamic 
Negative Correlation Learning (DNCL)” that dynamically 
adjusts the balance between diversity and accuracy during 
learning. In conventional methods, the parameters for 
adjusting this balance are fixed, making it difficult to respond 
flexibly according to the learning situation. On the other 
hand, DNCL aims to improve ensemble performance by 
dynamically adjusting parameters as learning progresses. 
Experiments on an image recognition task confirmed that 
DNCL outperforms conventional methods. Also, In the early 
stages of training, both accuracy and diversity were 
emphasized, and as training progressed, there was a tendency 
to place more emphasis on diversity. This trend was 
suggested to contribute to ensuring appropriate diversity 
among models. 
Keywords: Ensemble Learning, Negative Correlation 
Learning, Diversity. 
1. Introduction 
Deep ensemble learning is a technique that combines 
multiple deep learning models to achieve better 
generalization performance than a single model. Fig. 1.  
provides an overview of representative ensemble learning 
approaches. Fig. 1. (a) illustrates conventional single-model 
learning, Fig. 1. (b) shows traditional ensemble learning in 
which the loss is computed independently for each model, 
and Fig. 1. (c) depicts concatenated ensemble learning, 
where the loss is computed using the aggregated predictions 
of all models. Ensemble learning is advantageous because 
each model can compensate for others’ errors, thereby 
suppressing overfitting and improving robustness. However, 
if the predictions of individual models are highly similar, the 
benefit of ensembling becomes limited. Therefore, 
prediction diversity is a crucial factor for improving 
ensemble accuracy(1). 
Prediction diversity, characterized by different output 
distributions across models, is an important factor 
influencing the generalization performance of an ensemble. 
Nevertheless, the effects of diversity on accuracy are 
multifaceted, and the underlying mechanisms and optimal 
balance between diversity and accuracy remain 
insufficiently understood. Prior studies have shown that 
excessively prioritizing diversity can degrade performance 
in deep learning models(2), whereas low-capacity models 
benefit from high individual accuracy combined with 
sufficient diversity(3). Thus, achieving an appropriate balance 
between accuracy and diversity is essential. 
One approach addressing this challenge is Generalized 
Negative Correlation Learning (GNCL)(4). As illustrated in 
Fig. 1. (e), GNCL incorporates both the concatenated loss, 
representing the loss of the entire ensemble, and the 
distribution loss, representing the individual model losses, 
into a unified loss function. A weighting coefficient 𝜆  is 
introduced to control the balance between these two 
components, enabling a trade-off between ensemble 
accuracy and model diversity. However, GNCL employs a 
fixed 𝜆 throughout training, which poses two issues. First, 
the optimal value of 𝜆 depends on factors such as the task, 
model architecture, and dataset, making it difficult to 
determine in advance and increasing the tuning burden. 
Second, a fixed 𝜆 prevents adaptive adjustment of the loss 
balance according to training progress or model state, 
potentially limiting performance.

To address these issues, this study proposes Dynamic 
Negative Correlation Learning (DNCL), illustrated in Fig. 1. 
(f), which dynamically adjusts the weighting coefficient in 
the GNCL loss during training. By adapting  𝜆 according to 
the learning process, DNCL reduces the need for manual 
hyperparameter tuning while enabling flexible loss 
balancing that reflects the current training state. The novelty 
of this work lies in dynamically determining 𝜆, which was 
fixed in previous approaches. The objective of this study is 
to switch loss emphasis according to training dynamics, 
allowing each loss component to fulfill its role more 
effectively and thereby improving ensemble generalization 
performance. 
The contributions of this work are summarized as 
follows: 
 We propose DNCL, a new ensemble learning method 
that dynamically adjusts the weights of the 
concatenated loss and distribution loss, and we 
formally define the rule for determining the weighting 
coefficient. 
 We analyze the evolution of the weighting coefficient 
during training and reveal that its behavior varies 
depending on model capacity and task difficulty. 
 Through experiments on multiple datasets, we 
demonstrate that DNCL achieves higher generalization 
performance than existing methods that use a fixed 
weighting coefficient, thereby validating the 
effectiveness of dynamic loss balancing.

In Eq. (4), reducing 𝜆 emphasizes optimization based

which the models are trained separately. This independence 
naturally encourages diversity among models. Increasing 𝜆,

As described above, methods in the NCL family achieve 
model diversity by introducing a term that explicitly 
encourages the outputs of individual models to differ from 
one another.  
2.2 Concatenated Loss and Distribution Loss 
The Concatenated Loss is defined as the loss computed 
from the aggregated predictions of the ensemble. It directly 
optimizes the ensemble as a whole and plays a role in 
stabilizing the learning process. However, optimizing based 
on the Concatenated Loss can cause the individual models to 
adjust their predictions toward each other, since this 
minimizes the ensemble loss. As a result, the models may 
exhibit “Learner Collusion,” producing apparent diversity 
that does not contribute to generalization, ultimately 
increasing the risk of overfitting and degrading 
performance(6). Experimental comparisons between 
concatenated learning and independent learning using 
multiple deep neural networks have shown that independent 
learning yields consistently superior performance. 
Buschjäger et al. also reported that deep models possess 
strong representational capacity even individually, enabling 
them to fit the data well. Thus, concatenated learning can 
lead to excessive coordination, introducing redundancy and 
degrading performance(4). In contrast, for low-capacity 
models whose representational power is limited, cooperative 
behavior among models effectively increases the ensemble’s 
overall capacity, making concatenated learning beneficial for 
performance improvement. 
 The Distribution Loss, in contrast, is computed 
independently for each model based on its own predictions. 
By training each model separately, independent learning 
implicitly promotes diversity. This diversity largely arises 
from stochastic elements of deep learning, such as random 
initialization and mini-batch sampling order in SGD(6)(7). 
Furthermore, it has been suggested that the loss landscape of 
deep neural networks contains many distinct high-quality 
local minima(8). Independent learning thus allows each 
model to explore different solutions, contributing to the 
formation of an ensemble with natural and effective diversity.  
3. DNCL 
3.1 Problem Setting 
In this study, we focus on classification tasks and 
assume that a training dataset 𝐷=(𝑥௜,𝑦௜)௜ୀଵே   is given, 
where 𝑥௜ denotes the input data and 𝑦௜ its corresponding 
label. The proposed method does not impose any restrictions 
on the data type; although we describe the method using 
image classification as an example, it is applicable to general 
classification problems. 
We consider an ensemble learning framework composed 
of multiple deep learning models (ℎଵ, ℎଶ, … , ℎெ ) arranged 
in parallel. The models may share the same architecture or 
have different architectures, and they are trained using the 
same training dataset.

ensemble. Conversely, when the individual models have

training.

4. Experiments 
In this study, we investigate the following four Research 
Questions (RQ): 
(A) Does dynamically adjusting 𝜆  during training 
contribute to performance improvement? 
(B) Can DNCL be effectively applied across different 
model architectures and datasets? 
(C) To what extent does DNCL preserve model diversity? 
(D) Does large oscillation of 𝜆 in DNCL adversely affect 
performance? 
To address these questions, the subsequent sections 
evaluate DNCL and conventional methods across multiple 
model architectures and datasets. We first examine the 
training dynamics of 𝜆  and analyze the relationship 
between the Concatenated Loss and the Distribution Loss. 
We then quantify model diversity using established diversity 
metrics. Finally, we compare performance with and without 
the proposed oscillation-suppression techniques to 
determine whether fluctuations in 𝜆  negatively affect 
accuracy. 
4.1 Experimental Settings 
For the experiments, we employ the deep learning 
architectures ResNet18, EfficientNet-B0, DenseNet121, 
VGGNet16, MobileNetV3, and ResNeXt29. Training is 
conducted with SGD using an initial learning rate of 0.01, a 
batch size of 100, cosine annealing scheduling(9), 
CrossEntropyLoss, and 300 epochs. Ensembles are 
composed of four models sharing the same architecture. As 
datasets, we use CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100(10), two standard 
image classification benchmarks consisting of 10 and 100 
classes, respectively. Accuracy[%] is adopted as the 
evaluation metric.

lower performance compared to conventional methods.

to the baselines, indicating that understanding how to handle 
situations where the Concatenated Loss fails to converge 
provides an important direction for improving the stability of

issues, this study proposed DNCL, a method that 
dynamically adjusts 𝜆 in the GNCL loss function according 
to the relative contributions of the Concatenated Loss and the 
Distribution Loss. We evaluated the performance of DNCL 
in comparison with conventional methods. The results 
demonstrated that DNCL consistently outperformed existing 
approaches under various conditions. 
Visualization of the trajectory of 𝜆  during training 
revealed the following trend: in the early stage of training, 
DNCL places emphasis on both concatenated and 
independent learning; in the middle stage, the emphasis 
gradually shifts toward independent learning; and in the final 
stage, the model performs independent learning exclusively. 
These observations suggest that the type of learning that 
should be emphasized varies throughout training, and that 
DNCL successfully captures this transition by dynamically 
adjusting 𝜆. This adaptability likely contributes to DNCL’s 
superior performance compared to methods with fixed 𝜆. In 
contrast, when 𝜆  is fixed, the chosen value may not be 
optimal for all training stages, potentially limiting ensemble 
performance. 
Although DNCL suggests broader applicability to 
unexamined datasets and model architectures, its 
effectiveness under such conditions remains to be validated. 
In practical deployments such as industrial product 
inspection, additional constraints—including real-time 
inference and robustness to environmental noise—must be 
considered. Prior studies report improved performance 
through feature-space cooperation or model-specific 
weighting in ensembles. While these approaches enhance 
accuracy, they incur additional inference costs, leading to a 
trade-off between performance and computational efficiency 
that must be addressed. 
Furthermore, in this study, the parameter 𝜆 is adjusted 
solely based on the ratio between the concatenated loss and 
the distribution loss, and more rigorous adjustment strategies 
have not been explored. For instance, adapting 𝜆 based on 
the learning rate could allow a finer characterization of 
training dynamics. In addition, incorporating explicit 
diversity metrics into the adjustment rule may further 
improve ensemble performance. 
Future work will involve validating DNCL under 
additional learning conditions and investigating alternative 
mechanisms for dynamically controlling 𝜆.
