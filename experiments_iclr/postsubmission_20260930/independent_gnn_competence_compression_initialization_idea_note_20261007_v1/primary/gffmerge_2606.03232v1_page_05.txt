3.4. Targeted Fine-Tuning
While the closed-form merger yields a strong and well-
aligned initialization for the merged model, it does not fully
capture the representational capacity of the source models,
or in the presence of non-linear transformations. To re-
cover the remaining predictive fidelity, we introduce a brief
targeted fine-tuningstage.
Specifically, we performpartial fine-tuningof the later,
task-adaptive layers (such as energy or force heads, or any
non-linear layers) while keeping the analytically merged
early layers frozen. This design preserves the alignment
achieved by the closed-form solution, while allowing the
model to absorb residual discrepancies arising from non-
linear interactions and task-specific inductive biases.
4. Experiments
In this section, we present the first systematic benchmarking
study of model merging for GNN. Complete implementation
is available at https://github.com/idea-iitd/GFFMerge. Our
results establish:
• Efficacy:Existing model merging algorithms, developed
primarily for discrete prediction tasks on vision and text
modalities, suffer from severe performance degradation
when applied to force regression on atomistic systems.
GFFMERGEdirectly addresses this gap with dramatically
superior performance.
• Computational efficiency:GFFMERGEachieves up to
27× speedup, compared to standard Joint Fine-Tuning
of pre-trained force fields, while incurring only minor
losses in predictive accuracy. This efficiency stems from
an analytical merging procedure that exploits structural
properties of message-passing GNNforce fields, enabling
direct computation of merged parameters without costly
gradient-based optimization.
• Generality beyond force fields:Through extensive
benchmarking, we demonstrate that the proposed closed-
form model merging framework seamlessly extends to
standard GNNarchitectures such as GCN,GRAPHSAGE,
and graph transformers with efficiency gains of up to 3
orders of magnitude.
4.1. Experimental Setup
The details of our hardware and software environment are
listed in App. E. To ensure statistical meaningful results, all
experiments have been repeated thrice and the mean and
standard-deviation of the metric are reported.
Datasets & Evaluation Scenarios.We consider three com-
plementary datasets that differ in molecular size, structural
complexity, and interaction characteristics:
• MD17 (Small Molecules) (Chmiela et al., 2017):Or-
ganic molecules with well-defined local chemical environ-
ments, enabling controlled evaluation of merging force
fields. We evaluate model merging at multiple scales by
combining two, three, and five models, each trained on
distinct chemical systems.
• MD22 (Supramolecular Systems) (Chmiela et al.,
2023):Large peptides and molecular complexes with
long-range interactions and high conformational diver-
sity, testing the scalability of model merging to complex,
flexible systems.
• LiPS20 (Solid-State Electrolytes) (Chen et al., 2025):
Periodic crystalline systems exhibiting at high tempera-
tures and increased mobility, assessing the robustness of
merging in bulk systems.
Baselines and Gold Standard.We compare GFFMERGE
against several representative model-merging baselines, in-
cluding simple weight averaging, Fisher merging (Matena
& Raffel, 2022), Ties (Yadav et al., 2023), and EMR (Huang
et al., 2024). Notably, none of these methods are designed
for GNNs, and their evaluations have been largely restricted
to discrete prediction settings such as classification or next-
token prediction. We use the codebases released by the
respective authors.
As an upper bound, we report results for theGold Standard,
which corresponds to full fine-tuning (FT) of all parameters
of the foundation model on the union of the training datasets
from the source models, referred to as Joint FT.
Foundation Models.We evaluate GFFMERGEon two state-
of-the-art GNN force field foundation models, ORB(Neu-
mann et al., 2024) and M3GNET(Chen & Ong, 2022). To
assess generality beyond force fields, we additionally bench-
mark the method on standard GNNarchitectures, including
GCN, GIN, GAT, and GRAPHSAGE, across node classifi-
cation and link prediction (discussed in App. G). For these
